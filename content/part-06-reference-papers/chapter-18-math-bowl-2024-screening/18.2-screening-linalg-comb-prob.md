## Problem 3 (6 pts): Increasing Path of Length $\ge n - 1$ in $K_n$

**Statement**: The $\binom{n}{2}$ edges of the complete graph $K_n$ are given distinct weights $1, 2, \dots, \binom{n}{2}$. Prove there exists a path (edge-simple trail) of length at least $n - 1$ whose edge weights strictly increase.

> [!INTUITION]
> **The Famous Swapping-Walkers Idea (Graham & Kleitman, 1973)**
> Trying to extend one single increasing path greedily gets stuck in dead ends! Instead, imagine placing **$n$ tiny walkers**, one at each city of $K_n$. Call out the road labels $1, 2, 3, \dots$ in increasing order. Whenever road $uv$ is called, the two walkers currently standing at $u$ and $v$ walk across road $uv$ and switch places! Because road numbers strictly grow over time, every walker builds a strictly increasing trail automatically—and *how many total steps did all $n$ walkers take together?* Each of the $\binom{n}{2}$ roads moved $2$ walkers, giving $2\binom{n}{2} = n(n-1)$ steps shared among $n$ walkers!

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Move All $n$ Walkers at Once Instead of One Path</summary>

Don't try building one path! Imagine placing $n$ walkers (one per vertex) and activating edges $1, 2, \dots, \binom{n}{2}$ one by one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Track Total Steps Across All $n$ Walkers</summary>

Each time edge $t = uv$ turns on, swap the two walkers at $u$ and $v$. Every walker moves along strictly increasing edges—how many steps happen across all $n$ walkers combined?

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Position one walker on each of the $n$ vertices initially. Activate edges in ascending order $e_1, e_2, \dots, e_{\binom{n}{2}}$; when edge $e_t = uv$ activates, swap the walkers currently occupying endpoints $u$ and $v$.
> 1. Every time a walker steps across an active edge, its label $t$ exceeds all earlier edges used by that walker, so every walker traces a strictly increasing edge-simple **trail**.
> 2. Each edge moves $2$ walkers one step, so after all $\binom{n}{2}$ edges are processed, the sum of trail lengths across all $n$ walkers equals $2\binom{n}{2} = n(n - 1)$.
> 3. Because the average trail length across $n$ walkers is $\frac{n(n - 1)}{n} = n - 1$, by Pigeonhole at least one walker must achieve length $\ge n - 1$!
> *(Important distinction: in Graham–Kleitman / Screening P3, "path" means an edge-simple trail, because vertices are allowed to repeat. By contrast, demanding no repeated vertices on $K_4$ actually fails $48$ out of $720$ times—yielding max vertex-simple length $2$ while an increasing trail always reaches $3$!)*

## Problem 5 (6 pts): Matrix $2024 B = A^m + B^2$

**Statement**: Let $A$ be a real $n \times n$ matrix such that for every positive integer $m \ge 1$, there exists a real symmetric matrix $B$ obeying $2024 B = A^m + B^2$. Prove $|\det A| \le 1$.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Complete the Square on $2024 B - B^2$?**
> Rearranging gives $A^m = 2024 B - B^2$. Look at that quadratic $2024 b - b^2$ for ordinary numbers: completing the square writes $2024 b - b^2 = 1012^2 - (b - 1012)^2 \le 1012^2$! Does that same completing-the-square ceiling trap *matrix eigenvalues* too? Yes—because $B$ is real symmetric!

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Check What Symmetry Forces About $A$ and $(B - 1012 I)^2$</summary>

Write $A^m = 2024B - B^2$. Since $B$ is real symmetric, what does $m = 1$ immediately prove about $A$, and can $(B - 1012 I)^2$ ever have a negative eigenvalue?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Let $m$ Grow Arbitrarily Large</summary>

Since $A^m = 1012^2 I - (B - 1012 I)^2$, every eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda_i^m \le 1012^2$ for *every* $m \ge 1$. What happens for large even $m$ if $|\lambda_i| > 1$?

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> 1. **Symmetry of $A$**: Rewriting $A^m = 2024 B - B^2$, the right side is a polynomial in a real symmetric matrix $B$, so $A^m$ is **real symmetric** for every $m \ge 1$. Taking $m = 1$ proves $A$ itself is real symmetric, hence $A$ has real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ and an orthogonal eigenbasis.
> 2. **Completing the square**: Notice $(B - 1012 I)^2 = B^2 - 2024 B + 1012^2 I$, so:
>    $$A^m = 1012^2 I - (B - 1012 I)^2.$$
> 3. **Eigenvalue ceiling**: Any symmetric matrix squared is positive semidefinite ($x^{\mathsf{T}} M^2 x = \|Mx\|^2 \ge 0$), so all eigenvalues of $(B - 1012 I)^2$ are $\ge 0$. Therefore every eigenvalue $\lambda_i^m$ of $A^m$ satisfies $\lambda_i^m \le 1012^2$ for *every* $m \ge 1$!
> 4. If any $|\lambda_i| > 1$, taking a huge even integer $m = 2k$ forces $\lambda_i^{2k} = |\lambda_i|^{2k} \to \infty > 1012^2$, contradiction! Hence $|\lambda_i| \le 1$ for every $i$, giving $|\det A| = \prod_{i=1}^n |\lambda_i| \le 1$.

## Problem 6 (5 pts): Real Matrix $A + A^k = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ with Real Eigenvalues

**Statement**: Find all $n \times n$ real matrices $A$ having only real eigenvalues that satisfy $A + A^k = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ for some integer $k \ge n$.

**Answer**: Only the zero matrix $A = 0$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Use Real Schur Decomposition (Primer §0.4)</summary>

Because $A$ only has real eigenvalues, an orthogonal matrix $Q$ brings $T = Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q$ to upper triangular form with eigenvalues on the diagonal. Rewrite $A + A^k = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ in terms of $T$!

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Apply Cayley–Hamilton Since $k \ge n$</summary>

Comparing diagonal entries of $T + T^k = T^{\mathsf{T}}$ forces $\lambda_i^k = 0 \implies \lambda_i = 0$. Once $A$ is nilpotent ($A^n = 0 \implies A^k = 0$), what does $A = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ tell you about $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)$?

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Because every eigenvalue of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is real, the **Real Schur Decomposition** supplies an orthogonal matrix $Q$ ($Q^{\mathsf{T}} = Q^{-1}$) making $T = Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q$ **upper triangular**, with $A$'s eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ sitting down the main diagonal!
> Multiply $A + A^k = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ on the left by $Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ and on the right by $Q$:
> $$T + T^k = T^{\mathsf{T}}.$$
> Compare entry $(i, i)$ on the diagonal: $T^k$ has $\lambda_i^k$ at $(i, i)$, while $T^{\mathsf{T}}$ has the same diagonal $\lambda_i$! Thus:
> $$\lambda_i + \lambda_i^k = \lambda_i \implies \lambda_i^k = 0 \implies \lambda_i = 0 \quad \text{for every } i = 1, \dots, n.$$
> Having all eigenvalues $0$ makes $A$ **nilpotent**, so by Cayley–Hamilton ($A$'s characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^n = 0$), $A^n = 0$! Since $k \ge n$, we get $A^k = 0$, simplifying $A + A^k = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ down to $A = A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
> Finally, any symmetric matrix with all $0$ eigenvalues has $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \operatorname{tr}(A^2) = \sum \lambda_i^2 = 0$, which forces the sum of squares of *all* entries $\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}^2 = 0 \implies A = 0$!

## Problem 7 (6 pts): Rank-1 Commutator $\operatorname{rk}(AB - BA + I) = 1$

**Statement**: Let $n \times n$ real matrices $A, B$ satisfy $\operatorname{rk}(AB - BA + I) = 1$. Prove that $\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2) = \frac{1}{2}n(n - 1)$.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Write the Rank-1 Matrix Explicitly as $u v^{\mathsf{T}}$!**
> Any matrix of rank $1$ is simply an outer product $u v^{\mathsf{T}}$ of two column vectors $u, v$! Meanwhile, $\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$ looks suspiciously like half of $\operatorname{tr}\big((AB - BA)^2\big)$! Putting $C = AB - BA = u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I$ lets us calculate $\operatorname{tr}(C^2)$ in two lines.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Rewrite $\operatorname{rk}(C + I) = 1$ as $C = u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I$</summary>

Let $C = AB - BA$. Notice $\operatorname{tr}(C) = 0$, and express $C + I = u v^{\mathsf{T}}$ as a rank-1 outer product. What does $\operatorname{tr}(C) = 0$ immediately force on $v^{\mathsf{T}} u$?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Relate $\operatorname{tr}(C^2)$ to $\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$</summary>

Expand $C^2 = (AB - BA)^2$ and use cyclic trace invariance ($\operatorname{tr}(AB^2 A) = \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$), then square $C = u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I$ using $(u v^{\mathsf{T}})^2 = (v^{\mathsf{T}} u) u v^{\mathsf{T}}$!

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Let $C = AB - BA$. Since $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$, we immediately know $\operatorname{tr}(C) = 0$.
> Expand $C^2 = ABAB - AB^2 A - BA^2 B + BABA$ and take traces using cyclic invariance ($\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$, so $\operatorname{tr}(AB^2 A) = \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(BA^2 B) = \operatorname{tr}(B^2 A^2) = \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$):
> $$\operatorname{tr}(C^2) = 2\big(\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)\big).$$
> Next, because $\operatorname{rk}(C + I) = 1$, there exist column vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $C + I = u v^{\mathsf{T}}$, so $C = u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I$.
> Take the trace of $C$: $0 = \operatorname{tr}(u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I) = v^{\mathsf{T}} u - n \implies v^{\mathsf{T}} u = n$!
> Now square $C$ using $(u v^{\mathsf{T}})^2 = u (v^{\mathsf{T}} u) v^{\mathsf{T}} = n\, u v^{\mathsf{T}}$:
> $$C^2 = (u v^{\mathsf{T}} - I)^2 = n\, u v^{\mathsf{T}} - 2 u v^{\mathsf{T}} + I = (n - 2) u v^{\mathsf{T}} + I.$$
> Taking traces gives $\operatorname{tr}(C^2) = (n - 2)(v^{\mathsf{T}} u) + n = n(n - 2) + n = n(n - 1)$. Dividing by $2$ yields $\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2) = \frac{1}{2}n(n - 1)$!

## Problem 15 (8 pts): 1000 Copies of Letter Y on a Ruled Page

**Statement**: Toss $1000$ cardboard copies of the letter **Y** (three unit-length matchsticks meeting at $120^\circ$) onto parallel ruled lines spaced $3$ units apart. Compute: (a) the expected total number of intersections (**cuts**) with lines, and (b) the expected number of Ys hitting at least one line.

- **Part (a) Expected Total Cuts**: Each Y is built from $3$ unit segments, making $3000$ unit segments overall. By **Buffon's Noodle Theorem** (linearity of expectation holds *even though* the $3$ sticks of one Y are glued rigidly together!), a single unit needle ($L = 1$) dropped on spacing $d = 3$ has expected cuts $\frac{2L}{\pi d} = \frac{2}{3\pi}$. Across $3000$ sticks:
  $$\mathbb{E}[\text{cuts}] = 3000 \cdot \frac{2}{3\pi} = \frac{2000}{\pi} \approx 636.62.$$
- **Part (b) Expected Letters Hit**: A connected planar curve hits a straight line **if and only if** its **convex hull** intersects that line! Join the three tips of Y (two unit sticks at $120^\circ$ have tip-to-tip chord $2\sin 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$): the convex hull is an equilateral triangle of side $\sqrt{3}$ and total perimeter $L_{\text{hull}} = 3\sqrt{3}$. Because its width in any direction is at most $\sqrt{3} < 3$, the triangle can hit **at most one** ruled line! By **Crofton / Barbier's formula**, a convex shape of perimeter $P$ of diameter $< d$ crosses a ruled line with probability $\frac{P}{\pi d} = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{3\pi} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}$, yielding:
  $$\mathbb{E}[\text{letters hit}] = \frac{1000\sqrt{3}}{\pi} \approx 551.33.$$

## Problem 17 (3 pts): Variance of Fixed Points in Hat-Matching

**Statement**: $N$ guests check $N$ hats and receive them back uniformly at random. Let $S$ count how many guests receive their own hat. Find $\operatorname{Var}(S)$.

> [!PROOF]
> For $N = 1$, $S$ is identically $1$, so $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 0$. Now suppose $N \ge 2$, and write $S = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$ where $X_i = 1$ if guest $i$ gets their own hat and $0$ otherwise.
> 1. Each single guest has chance $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{N}$, so $\mathbb{E}[S] = N \cdot \frac{1}{N} = 1$. Since $X_i^2 = X_i$, we also have $\mathbb{E}[X_i^2] = \frac{1}{N}$.
> 2. Pick any **ordered pair** of distinct guests $i \neq j$: for *both* to get their own hats, guest $i$ gets theirs ($1/N$) and conditional on that, guest $j$ gets theirs among the remaining $N - 1$ hats ($1/(N-1)$), giving $\mathbb{E}[X_i X_j] = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}$.
> 3. Expand $S^2$:
> $$\mathbb{E}[S^2] = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] + \sum_{i \neq j} \mathbb{E}[X_i X_j] = N \cdot \frac{1}{N} + N(N - 1) \cdot \frac{1}{N(N - 1)} = 1 + 1 = 2.$$
> Subtract $\mathbb{E}[S]^2 = 1^2$: $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 2 - 1 = \mathbf{1}$—completely independent of $N \ge 2$!

## Problem 18 (8 pts): Simultaneous Rumor Spread on a 6-Vertex Graph

**Statement**: On the $6$-vertex graph with edges `0-2, 0-4, 0-5, 1-4, 1-5, 2-3, 2-4, 4-5` (notice $8$ edges total!), nodes `0, 1` start **White** and `2, 3, 4, 5` start **Black**. Simultaneously each round, every vertex flips a fair coin: Heads keeps its own color; Tails copies a uniformly chosen neighbor's color. Find the exact probability that the entire graph turns all-White.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Weight Vertices by Degree $\deg(v)$ to Make a Martingale?**
> If every vertex changed color directly with equal probability, unweighted white counts wouldn't stay balanced because high-degree hubs influence many neighbors! Look at edge $uv$: vertex $u$ copies $v$ with rate $\frac{1}{2\deg(u)}$, while $v$ copies $u$ with rate $\frac{1}{2\deg(v)}$. Multiplying vertex $u$'s indicator by **$\deg(u)$** cancels those denominators out so every edge contributes identically in both directions!

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Find the Exact Weighting That Cancels Denominators</summary>

At a single edge $uv$, $u$ adopts $v$'s color with probability $\frac{1}{2\deg(u)}$, while $v$ adopts $u$'s with probability $\frac{1}{2\deg(v)}$. What weight $w_v$ makes $w_u \cdot \frac{1}{\deg(u)} = w_v \cdot \frac{1}{\deg(v)}$?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Apply Optional Stopping at Consensus (Primer §0.4)</summary>

Let $M_t = \sum_v \deg(v) X_v(t)$, check $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t$, and equate $\mathbb{E}[M_\infty] = p \cdot 2|E| + (1 - p) \cdot 0 = M_0$!

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Let $X_v(t) \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate whether vertex $v$ is White at step $t$. In one step, conditional on state $\mathcal{F}_t$, $\mathbb{E}[X_v(t+1) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \frac{1}{2}X_v(t) + \frac{1}{2\deg(v)}\sum_{u \sim v} X_u(t)$.
> Form the **degree-weighted score** $M_t = \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)\, X_v(t)$:
> $$\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{v} \deg(v) X_v(t) + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{v}\sum_{u \sim v} X_u(t) = \frac{1}{2}M_t + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{u} \deg(u) X_u(t) = M_t.$$
> So $M_t$ is an exact **martingale**! Because the $1/2$ lazy self-stay prevents periodic bouncing, the finite Markov chain reaches consensus (all-White $M_\infty = 2|E|$ or all-Black $M_\infty = 0$) with probability $1$. By Optional Stopping ($\mathbb{E}[M_\infty] = M_0$):
> $$P(\text{all-White}) = \frac{M_0}{2|E|} = \frac{\deg(0) + \deg(1)}{\sum_{v=0}^5 \deg(v)} = \frac{3 + 2}{3 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 4 + 3} = \frac{5}{16}.$$

## Python Verification of Problems 17 & 18

Let us run an exact check over all $6! = 720$ permutations for Problem 17 and an $8000$-trial Markov simulation of Problem 18 in Python:

```python
import math, random

# Check Problem 17 (N=7) & Problem 18 (6-vertex rumor graph)
random.seed(123)
adj = {0: [2,4,5], 1: [4,5], 2: [0,3,4], 3: [2], 4: [0,1,2,5], 5: [0,1,4]}
degs = {v: len(adj[v]) for v in adj}
print("P18 exact (deg0+deg1)/2|E| =", (degs[0]+degs[1])/sum(degs.values()))

wins = 0
sims = 8000
for _ in range(sims):
    col = {0: 1, 1: 1, 2: 0, 3: 0, 4: 0, 5: 0}
    while len(set(col.values())) > 1:
        col = {v: (col[v] if random.random() < 0.5 else col[random.choice(adj[v])]) for v in col}
    wins += col[0]
print(f"P18 Monte Carlo ({sims} trials): {wins/sims:.4f} (exact 5/16 = {5/16:.4f})")

# Check Problem 17 variance for N=6 across all 6! = 720 permutations
from itertools import permutations
counts = [sum(1 for i, p in enumerate(perm) if i == p) for perm in permutations(range(6))]
mean = sum(counts) / len(counts)
var = sum((c - mean)**2 for c in counts) / len(counts)
print(f"P17 exact over S_6: E[S]={mean}, Var(S)={var}")
```

```output
P18 exact (deg0+deg1)/2|E| = 0.3125
P18 Monte Carlo (8000 trials): 0.3161 (exact 5/16 = 0.3125)
P17 exact over S_6: E[S]=1.0, Var(S)=1.0
```

2. In Problem 18, what happens to the all-White probability if ONLY vertex `4` starts White and all five other vertices start Black?

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Self-Check Exercises</summary>

1. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$! Wait: $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $C = AB - BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$—wait: $C + I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which has **rank $1$**!! Let's check $\operatorname{tr}(ABAB) - \operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2)$: $(AB)^2 = 0 \implies \operatorname{tr} = 0$, while $A^2 = A$ and $B^2 = -I$, so $\operatorname{tr}(A^2 B^2) = \operatorname{tr}(-A) = -1$! Giving $0 - (-1) = \mathbf{1} = \frac{1}{2}(2)(1)$! Exact!
2. Using our martingale formula, $M_0 = \deg(4) = 4$, and total $2|E| = 16$, so $P(\text{all-White}) = \frac{4}{16} = \frac{1}{4}$—higher than vertex `3`'s $\frac{1}{16}$ because vertex `4` has four times as many neighbors!

</details>
