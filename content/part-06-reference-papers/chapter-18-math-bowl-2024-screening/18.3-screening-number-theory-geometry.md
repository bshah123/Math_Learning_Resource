## Problem 2 (5 pts): Maximizing $\angle OPA$ Inside a Circle

**Statement**: Let $O$ be the center of a circle of radius $R$ and let $A$ be a fixed interior point with $0 < OA = d < R$. Find all points $P$ on the circumference maximizing angle $\angle OPA$.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Link the Unknown Angle $\angle OPA$ Directly to $\sin \angle OAP$ via Law of Sines!**
> As $P$ slides around the circle, $OP = R$ and $OA = d$ never change! In $\triangle OAP$, **Law of Sines** connects two constant sides ($OA, OP$) straight to the two opposite angles ($\angle OPA, \angle OAP$)! Wait: could $\angle OPA$ ever become obtuse and fool us? Notice $OA = d < R = OP$, and in any triangle the smaller side always faces the smaller angle—so $\angle OPA$ is trapped strictly below $90^\circ$, where $\sin$ increases strictly!

> [!PROOF]
> Applying the Law of Sines inside $\triangle OAP$ gives:
> $$\frac{\sin \angle OPA}{OA} = \frac{\sin \angle OAP}{OP} \implies \sin \angle OPA = \frac{d}{R}\sin \angle OAP.$$
> Because $OA = d < R = OP$, $\angle OPA < \angle OAP$, so $\angle OPA$ cannot be obtuse or right—it stays strictly in $(0, \pi/2)$. On $(0, \pi/2)$, $\sin\theta$ strictly increases with $\theta$, so maximizing $\angle OPA$ is identical to maximizing $\sin \angle OAP$, whose upper limit is $1$ at $\angle OAP = 90^\circ$!
> Thus the unique maximizers are the **two endpoints** of the chord passing through $A$ perpendicular to segment $OA$ (achieving $\sin \angle OPA_{\max} = d/R$).

## Problem 8 (4 pts): Totient Divisor Sum $\sum_{k=1}^n \lfloor n/k \rfloor \varphi(k)$

**Statement**: Prove for every positive integer $n \ge 1$ that $\sum_{k=1}^n \left\lfloor \frac{n}{k} \right\rfloor \varphi(k) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Double Counting Multiples Unlocks Gauss's Totient Identity $\sum_{k \mid m} \varphi(k) = m$!**
> Whenever you see $\lfloor n/k \rfloor$ multiplying an arithmetic function $f(k)$, ask: *what does $\lfloor n/k \rfloor$ count?* Literally how many integers $m \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ are divisible by $k$! Swap the sums over $k$ and $m$—just like row-vs-column double counting in Lesson 3.1!

> [!PROOF]
> Rewrite $\lfloor n/k \rfloor$ as a sum of $1$'s over every multiple $m \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ of $k$, then interchange summation order:
> $$\sum_{k=1}^n \left\lfloor \frac{n}{k} \right\rfloor \varphi(k) = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{\substack{1 \le m \le n \\ k \mid m}} \varphi(k) = \sum_{m=1}^n \left(\sum_{k \mid m} \varphi(k)\right).$$
> By Euler's classic totient identity (every fraction $a/m$ reduces to a unique denominator $d = m/\gcd(a,m)$ with $\varphi(d)$ numerators, summing across $k \mid m$ to $m$), the inner sum equals $m$! Therefore:
> $$\sum_{m=1}^n m = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}.$$

## Problem 12 (5 pts): LCM Lower Bound $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) + \operatorname{lcm}(m+1,n+1) > \frac{2mn}{\sqrt{m-n}}$

**Statement**: For all integers $m > n \ge 1$, prove $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) + \operatorname{lcm}(m+1, n+1) > \frac{2mn}{\sqrt{m-n}}$.

> [!PROOF]
> Write $\operatorname{lcm}(x, y) = \frac{xy}{\gcd(x, y)}$, setting $d_1 = \gcd(m, n)$ and $d_2 = \gcd(m+1, n+1)$. Notice two clean facts about $d_1$ and $d_2$:
> 1. $d_1$ divides both $m$ and $n$, so $d_1 \mid (m - n)$. Likewise $d_2$ divides $(m+1) - (n+1) = m - n$, so $d_2 \mid (m - n)$.
> 2. Any common divisor of $d_1$ and $d_2$ divides both $m$ and $m + 1$ (consecutive integers!), forcing $\gcd(d_1, d_2) = 1$!
> Since $d_1$ and $d_2$ are coprime divisors of $m - n$, their **product** divides $m - n$: $d_1 d_2 \mid (m - n) \implies d_1 d_2 \le m - n$.
> Now apply $(m+1)(n+1) > mn$ and **AM-GM** ($x + y \ge 2\sqrt{xy}$):
> $$\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) + \operatorname{lcm}(m+1, n+1) = \frac{mn}{d_1} + \frac{(m+1)(n+1)}{d_2} > \frac{mn}{d_1} + \frac{mn}{d_2} \ge \frac{2mn}{\sqrt{d_1 d_2}} \ge \frac{2mn}{\sqrt{m - n}}.$$

## Problem 13 (7 pts): Shifted Harmonic Sums Never Equal an Integer

**Statement**: Prove that $\frac{1}{m} + \frac{1}{m+1} + \dots + \frac{1}{m+n}$ is never an integer for any positive integers $m, n \ge 1$.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Spot the Unique Denominator With the Highest Power of $2$!**
> Recall from Lesson 9.1 why $1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$ isn't an integer: we grabbed a Bertrand prime $p$ appearing in just one denominator! Here our interval $[m, m+n]$ might avoid primes, yet **$2$ works even better without needing Bertrand**: between any two multiples of $2^r$, there sits an odd-vs-even jump creating a multiple of $2^{r+1}$!

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Check Why $2^r$ Beats Hunting for a Bertrand Prime</summary>

Unlike $1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$, an arbitrary block $[m, m+n]$ might not contain a prime $> (m+n)/2$. Instead, look at the **highest power of $2$**, say $2^r$, dividing any denominator in the block!

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Show Only One Term Can Carry $2^r$</summary>

Suppose $2^r q$ and $2^r q'$ ($q < q'$) both lie in $[m, m+n]$. Why must $2^r(q + 1)$ or $2^r q$ actually be divisible by $2^{r+1}$?

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Look at all $n + 1 \ge 2$ denominators `{m, m+1, ..., m+n}` and let $2^r$ be the **highest power of $2$** dividing any number in that block (since there are at least two consecutive numbers, at least one is even, so $r \ge 1$).
> Could **two** distinct integers $a < b$ in $[m, m+n]$ both be divisible by $2^r$? Suppose $a = 2^r q$ and $b = 2^r q'$ ($q < q'$). Then $2^r(q + 1)$ also lies inside $[m, m+n]$, and between the consecutive integers $q$ and $q + 1$, one must be **even**! Thus either $a$ or $2^r(q+1)$ is divisible by **$2^{r+1}$**, contradicting our choice of $r$ as the highest power!
> Hence **exact one** denominator in $[m, m+n]$ carries $2^r$, say $2^r C$ ($C$ odd). Combine all other terms into one fraction $\frac{A}{2^{r-1} B}$ (where $B$ is odd):
> $$\sum_{k=m}^{m+n} \frac{1}{k} = \frac{A}{2^{r-1} B} + \frac{1}{2^r C} = \frac{2AC + B}{2^r BC}.$$
> Since $B$ is odd, the numerator $2AC + B$ is **odd**, while the denominator $2^r BC$ is **even** ($r \ge 1$)—so the sum can never reduce to an integer!

## Problem 14 (7 pts): Monotonic Divisor-Count Functional Equation

**Statement**: Find all functions $f : \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \to \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ such that for all $a \le b$, $f(a) \le f(b)$ and $\tau(f(a) + f(b)) = \tau(a + b)$ (where $\tau(k)$ counts positive divisors of $k$).

**Answer**: The unique solution is $f(n) = n$ for all $n \ge 1$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Pin $f(1) = 1$, Then Break Any Flat Tie $f(n) = f(n+1)$</summary>

Set $a = b = 1$ to get $\tau(2 f(1)) = 2$. Next, if $f(n) = f(n + 1)$, varying $a \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ forces $\tau$ to stay constant on all of $[n + 1, 2n + 1]$—how does **Bertrand's Postulate** blow that up?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Squeeze From Above Using Odd Primes</summary>

Once $f(n) \ge n$ everywhere, plug $a = b = 2^{p-2}$ for any odd prime $p$. What numbers have a *prime* divisor count $\tau(2 f(2^{p-2})) = p$?

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> 1. **Pinning $f(1) = 1$**: Plug $a = b = 1$ into $\tau(f(a) + f(b)) = \tau(a + b)$: $\tau(2 f(1)) = \tau(2) = 2$. Only prime numbers have $2$ divisors, and the only even prime is $2$, forcing $2 f(1) = 2 \implies f(1) = 1$.
> 2. **Strict monotonicity via Bertrand's Postulate (Lesson 9.1)**: Suppose two adjacent values tie: $f(n) = f(n+1)$ for some $n \ge 1$. For $n = 1$, $f(2) = 1$ gives $\tau(f(2) + f(2)) = \tau(2) = 2$ vs $\tau(4) = 3$, false! For $n \ge 2$, test every $a \in \{1, \dots, n\}$:
>    $$\tau(a + n) = \tau(f(a) + f(n)) = \tau(f(a) + f(n+1)) = \tau(a + n + 1).$$
>    This forces $\tau(k)$ to be **constant** across the entire block $[n + 1, 2n + 1]$! Yet by **Bertrand's Postulate** on $n + 1$, there exists a prime $p \in (n + 1, 2n + 1]$ (where $\tau(p) = 2$), whereas the interval also contains even integers $> 2$ (where $\tau \ge 3$), contradiction! Hence $f(1) < f(2) < \dots$, implying $f(n) \ge n$ for all $n \ge 1$.
> 3. **Pinning infinitely many upper milestones via odd primes**: Take any odd prime $p \ge 3$ and plug $a = b = 2^{p-2}$: $\tau(2 f(2^{p-2})) = \tau(2^{p-1}) = p$. A number has a *prime* number $p$ of divisors if and only if it is the $(p-1)$-th power of a single prime ($q^{p-1}$)! Since $2$ divides $2 f(2^{p-2})$, that prime $q$ must be $2$, forcing $2 f(2^{p-2}) = 2^{p-1} \implies f(2^{p-2}) = 2^{p-2}$!
> 4. Since $f$ is strictly increasing with $f(1) = 1$ and $f(2^{p-2}) = 2^{p-2}$ for arbitrarily large primes $p$, every integer between $1$ and $2^{p-2}$ is squeezed tightly into **$f(n) = n$**!

## Problem 16 (5 pts): $\prod_{i=1}^{2016} (i^4 + 5) \pmod{2017}$

**Statement**: Determine the remainder when $\prod_{i=1}^{2016} (i^4 + 5)$ is divided by $2017$.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Group Fourth Powers in $\mathbb{F}_{2017}^*$ (Primer §0.4)</summary>

Note $2017$ is prime and $2017 - 1 = 4 \cdot 504$. As $i$ runs through $1 \dots 2016$, how many times does $y = i^4 \bmod 2017$ visit each element of the 504-element subgroup $H = \{y : y^{504} \equiv 1\}$?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Polynomial Factorization + Quadratic Reciprocity</summary>

Factor $\prod_{y \in H} (T + y) \equiv T^{504} - 1$ (since $504$ is even!), set $T = 5$, and use $\left(\frac{5}{2017}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{5}\right) = -1$ to find $(5^{504})^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{2017}$!

</details>

> [!PROOF]
> Notice first that $2017$ is prime, and $2017 - 1 = 2016 = 4 \cdot 504$. As $i$ runs across the $2016$ nonzero residues $\mathbb{F}_{2017}^*$, what values does $y = i^4$ take?
> Every $y = i^4$ obeys $y^{504} \equiv i^{2016} \equiv 1 \pmod{2017}$, and because $\mathbb{F}_{2017}^*$ is cyclic of order $2016$, there are exactly $504$ fourth-power roots of unity in the subgroup $H = \{y : y^{504} = 1\}$, with **each $y \in H$ hit by $i^4$ exactly $4$ times**!
> Notice also that $504$ is even, so $(-y)^{504} = y^{504}$—meaning the polynomial $T^{504} - 1 \equiv \prod_{y \in H} (T - y)$ equals $\prod_{y \in H} (T + y) \pmod{2017}$ too! Plug in $T = 5$:
> $$\prod_{i=1}^{2016} (i^4 + 5) \equiv \left(\prod_{y \in H} (5 + y)\right)^4 \equiv (5^{504} - 1)^4 \pmod{2017}.$$
> Now evaluate $w = 5^{504} \bmod 2017$: by Quadratic Reciprocity ($2017 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$), $\left(\frac{5}{2017}\right) = \left(\frac{2017}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{5}\right) = -1$, so $5^{1008} = w^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{2017}$! Expand $(w - 1)^4$:
> $$(w - 1)^4 = (w^2 - 2w + 1)^2 \equiv (-1 - 2w + 1)^2 = (-2w)^2 = 4w^2 \equiv -4 \equiv \mathbf{2013} \pmod{2017}.$$

## Python Verification

Let us check Problem 8 for $n = 1, 5, 10$ and compute the full modular product of Problem 16 directly in Python:

```python
import math

# Verify Problem 8 for n = 1..10
def phi(k):
    return sum(1 for x in range(1, k + 1) if math.gcd(x, k) == 1)

for n in [1, 5, 10]:
    lhs = sum((n // k) * phi(k) for k in range(1, n + 1))
    print(f"P8 n={n:2d}: sum = {lhs}, n(n+1)/2 = {n*(n+1)//2}")

# Verify Problem 16 exact product mod 2017
prod = 1
for i in range(1, 2017):
    prod = (prod * (pow(i, 4, 2017) + 5)) % 2017
print("P16 product mod 2017 =", prod)
```

```output
P8 n= 1: sum = 1, n(n+1)/2 = 1
P8 n= 5: sum = 15, n(n+1)/2 = 15
P8 n=10: sum = 55, n(n+1)/2 = 55
P16 product mod 2017 = 2013
```
