In normal games, players patiently take alternating turns (`Left, Right, Left, Right...`). Now change one rule: **at the start of every single turn, both players secretly bid money for the right to move!** Even more surprisingly, whoever wins the auction pays their bid *directly into the loser's pocket*! So winning too often leaves your opponent rich enough to buy every remaining turn.

How much money $R(v)$ do you need at position $v$ to guarantee a win? Under continuous money (**Richman bidding**), the answer turns out to match an ordinary coin-flip game! And when money comes as indivisible whole chips plus a **tie-break token**, counting each chip as $2$ half-units and the token as $1$ unlocks exact parity bounds cleanly.

## 1. Richman Games With Continuous Money

Imagine a token sitting on a finite acyclic directed graph (no endless cycles). Total money in circulation is $1$, with **Left** holding $x \in [0, 1]$ and **Right** holding $1 - x$. At each turn:
1. Both players secretly write down nonnegative bids not exceeding their current cash.
2. The higher bidder takes that bid out of their wallet, hands it directly to the opponent, and slides the token to any legal successor $w \in S(v)$.
3. Left wins immediately upon reaching target $t_L$; Right wins upon reaching $t_R$.

Wait—how should we assign a "price" $R(v)$ to every vertex? Look at the best move Left wants (the cheapest successor, costing $\min_{w} R(w)$) vs the worst move Right could force (the most expensive successor, costing $\max_{w} R(w)$). The fair threshold sits cleanly halfway between them:

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Richman Cost Function $R(v)$**
> Define $R(t_L) = 0$ (Left needs no money—already won!), $R(t_R) = 1$ (Right already won!), and recursively backward on every other vertex $v$:
> $$R(v) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{w \in S(v)} R(w) + \min_{w \in S(v)} R(w)\right).$$

> [!THEOREM]
> **Richman Threshold Theorem (Lazarus, Loeb, Propp, Ullman)**
> If Left strictly holds more than the Richman price ($x > R(v)$), Left can guarantee victory from $v$. If $x < R(v)$, Right guarantees victory. At exact equality $x = R(v)$, whoever wins tie-breaks decides.

> [!PROOF]
> Suppose Left currently stands at $v$ with cash $x > R(v)$. Let $R^+ = \max_{w \in S(v)} R(w)$ be the worst successor and $R^- = \min_{w \in S(v)} R(w)$ be the best successor, so $R(v) = \frac{R^+ + R^-}{2}$.
> Tell Left to bid **exact midpoint half-gap** $\beta = \frac{R^+ - R^-}{2}$ (notice $\beta \le R(v) < x$, so Left always has enough cash!). Watch what happens either way:
> - **Case 1: Left wins the bid**. Left pays $\beta$ to Right and moves to the minimum-cost vertex $w^-$. Left's remaining money is $x - \beta > R(v) - \frac{R^+ - R^-}{2} = \frac{R^+ + R^- - R^+ + R^-}{2} = R^- = R(w^-)$!
> - **Case 2: Left loses the bid**. Right must have bid at least $\beta$, so Right hands $\ge \beta$ over to Left! Left's new cash is at least $x + \beta > R(v) + \frac{R^+ - R^-}{2} = R^+$, which strictly beats *every* successor $w \in S(v)$ Right could possibly move to!
> In both cases, Left's invariant $x_{\text{new}} > R(v_{\text{new}})$ holds tightly at the next vertex! Because the graph has no cycles, play must terminate—and since Left's cash always stays strictly positive ($> R(v) \ge 0$), Left can *never* land on $t_R$ ($R(t_R) = 1 > 1$ impossible!), so Left wins at $t_L$!

> [!INTUITION]
> **The Amazing Fair-Coin Equivalence**
> Look again at the equation $R(v) = \frac{1}{2} R^+ + \frac{1}{2} R^-$. That is the **exact probability** that Left loses from $v$ if instead of bidding money, a fair 50/50 coin were flipped every turn to decide who gets to move (with both players picking optimal arrows)! Check it on a simple best-of race: suppose Left needs $2$ wins before Right gets $3$ wins! With fair coin tosses, Left wins overall iff Left flips at least $2$ Heads in $4$ coin tosses: $P(\text{win}) = \frac{\binom{4}{2} + \binom{4}{3} + \binom{4}{4}}{16} = \frac{11}{16}$, so Left's losing chance is $1 - \frac{11}{16} = \frac{5}{16}$—matching the Richman recursion $R(0,0) = 5/16$ to the last fraction! *(Contrast **poorman** bidding, where bids go to an outside bank rather than the opponent, shifting thresholds.)*

## 2. Whole Chips: Counting in Half-Units + Tie-Break Token

Now replace continuous dollars with **$N$ discrete whole chips** shared between Left ($a$ chips) and Right ($N - a$ chips). Because ties happen constantly with integers, introduce a **tie-break token**: on equal bids, the token holder wins the right to move, pays their bid chips, and **hands the tie-break token over to the opponent**!

How much is that tie-break token really worth? Notice it beats an equal bid by less than $1$ full chip, yet strictly beats having zero extra power! Calling **each chip $2$ units** and the **tie-break token $1$ unit** makes Left's effective wealth:
$$u = 2a + t \qquad (t = 1 \text{ if Left holds the token, } 0 \text{ otherwise}).$$
Look at the magic of $2N + 1$ total units: **$2N + 1$ is always odd**, so Left and Right can *never* tie in total half-units!

- **Final-round rule** (next turn wins the whole game): Left wins if and only if $u \ge N + 1$ (holding strictly more than half of the $2N + 1$ total units!). *Check why*: if $a > N - a$, Left bets $N - a + 1$ or more and cannot be matched; if $N$ is even and $a = N - a = N/2$, Left wins all-in iff $t = 1$ ($u = N + 1$)! Whereas for odd $N$, strict chip majority already decides the last turn regardless of the token.
- **First-to-2-wins race** (verified for all $N \le 40$): Notice how whole-chip thresholds follow the exact continuous coin fractions ($1/2, 1/4, 3/4$), shifted by one half-unit!

| Current Match Score | Left Wins With Discrete Chips Iff | Continuous Richman Fraction |
|---|---|---|
| $1$–$1$ (decider) | $u \ge N + 1$ | $1/2$ |
| Left leads $1$–$0$ | $2u \ge N$ | $1/4$ |
| Right leads $0$–$1$ | $2u \ge 3N + 3$ | $3/4$ |
| $0$–$0$ (start) | $u \ge N + 1$ | $1/2$ |

In exact competition terms: the tie-break advantage token is literally worth **half a chip**!

## 3. Raise Ladders: Arithmetic Modulo $m + 1$

Another classic bidding format drops hidden bids completely: two buyers take turns publicly raising the auction price starting from $\$0$ by any integer step from $1$ up to $m$. Whoever pushes the price to exact target $T$ wins!

Just like bounded Nim, classify target gaps backwards modulo $m + 1$: if the gap remaining to $T$ is a multiple of $m + 1$, whatever raise $r \in [1, m]$ you pick, your opponent immediately answers with $(m + 1 - r) \in [1, m]$ to land right back on a multiple of $m + 1$—and ultimately claims $T$! Hence **Player 1 wins if and only if $T \not\equiv 0 \pmod{m + 1}$**, opening with $T \bmod (m+1)$. (For $m = 1$, steps of $\$1$ only: Player 1 wins iff $T$ is odd!)

## 4. Python Richman & Chip Threshold Verifier

Let us compute the `2-vs-3` Richman race threshold and compare whole-chip half-unit cutoffs for $N = 8$ (even) and $N = 9$ (odd) in Python:

```python
from functools import lru_cache

# 1. Verify Richman race R(0, 0) = 5/16 for Left needing 2 wins vs Right needing 3 wins
@lru_cache(None)
def richman_race(i, j):
    if i == 2:
        return 0.0
    if j == 3:
        return 1.0
    return 0.5 * (richman_race(i + 1, j) + richman_race(i, j + 1))

print(f"Richman R(0, 0) = {richman_race(0, 0)} (5/16 = {5/16})")

# 2. Check whole-chip winning thresholds for N=8 (even) and N=9 (odd)
for N in (8, 9):
    rows = [(a, t, 2*a + t) for a in range(N + 1) for t in (0, 1)]
    min_1_1 = min(u for _, _, u in rows if u >= N + 1)
    min_1_0 = min(u for _, _, u in rows if 2*u >= N)
    min_0_1 = min(u for _, _, u in rows if 2*u >= 3*N + 3)
    print(f"N={N}: min u for 1-1={min_1_1}, 1-0={min_1_0}, 0-1={min_0_1} (total units={2*N+1})")
```

```output
Richman R(0, 0) = 0.3125 (5/16 = 0.3125)
N=8: min u for 1-1=9, 1-0=4, 0-1=14 (total units=17)
N=9: min u for 1-1=10, 1-0=5, 0-1=15 (total units=19)
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Remember: Richman Bids Stay Inside the Game!**
> Unlike real-world auctions where payments go to an auctioneer, a winning bid in a Richman game transfers directly to your **opponent's** stack. Overbidding doesn't just drain your wallet—it hands ammunition straight to your rival!
