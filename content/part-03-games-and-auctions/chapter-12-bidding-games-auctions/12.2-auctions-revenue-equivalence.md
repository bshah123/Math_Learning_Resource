Suppose you are auctioning off a rare comic book to $n$ collectors, and each collector privately knows how much the comic is worth to them—modeled as independent uniform draws $v_i \sim \operatorname{Uniform}[0, 1]$. Naturally you start comparing auction formats to maximize your payout:
- Should the winner pay their **own** sealed bid (**First-Price**)?
- Should the winner only pay the **second-highest** bid (**Vickrey Second-Price**)?
- Or should **everyone** forfeit their bids regardless of whether they win or lose (**All-Pay**)?

At first glance, making *everyone* pay—or letting winners pay less than their own bid—sounds completely different! Yet one of the crown jewels of economic theory proves that in equilibrium, **all three formats raise the exact same average money for the seller**!

## 1. Four Classic Auction Formats Side by Side

| Auction Format | What the Winner Pays | Optimal Equilibrium Bid $b(v)$ | Expected Seller Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Second-price (Vickrey)** | Second-highest bid | $b(v) = v$ (honest!) | $\frac{n - 1}{n + 1}$ |
| **First-price sealed** | Own bid | $b(v) = \frac{n - 1}{n} v$ (shaded!) | $\frac{n - 1}{n + 1}$ |
| **All-pay sealed** | Own bid (even losers pay!) | $b(v) = \frac{n - 1}{n} v^n$ | $\frac{n - 1}{n + 1}$ |
| **Uniform-price ($k$ identical items, $1$ per person)** | $(k+1)$-th highest bid per item | $b(v) = v$ (honest!) | $\frac{k(n - k)}{n + 1}$ |

### Why Does Honesty Win in Vickrey (Second-Price) Auctions?

Suppose the comic is worth $v = \$50$ to you, and you write down a sealed bid in a second-price auction. Should you ever bid $\$60$ or $\$40$ instead of $\$50$?
- **Overbidding at $\$60$**: Never affects the outcome unless the highest rival bid $M$ lands strictly between $\$50$ and $\$60$—and in that exact case, bidding $\$60$ makes you win the comic for price $M > \$50$, losing money compared to staying out!
- **Underbidding at $\$40$**: Only changes anything if $M$ lands between $\$40$ and $\$50$—forcing you to miss out on a bargain priced below your true $\$50$ valuation!
Therefore **bidding your exact value $b(v) = v$ is always a weakly dominant strategy**! Since everyone bids truthfully, the price equals the **$(n-1)$-th smallest** out of $n$ Uniform$[0, 1]$ values, whose average by Toolkit Lesson 0.2 (order statistics!) is:
$$\mathbb{E}[V_{(n-1)}] = \frac{n - 1}{n + 1}.$$

### Why Shaded First-Price Bids Hit the Exact Same Revenue!

In a **first-price** auction, bidding your full value $v$ leaves zero profit ($v - v = 0$), so everyone shades their bid below $v$. Suppose rivals bid $cw$. If your bid $\beta$ beats all $n - 1$ independent rivals (which happens when every rival has $v_i < \beta / c$, probability $(\beta/c)^{n-1}$), your expected profit is $(v - \beta)(\beta/c)^{n-1}$. Differentiating with respect to $\beta$ and setting to $0$ yields $\beta = \frac{n - 1}{n} v$, confirming $c = \frac{n - 1}{n}$! Now check average revenue: the highest value has average $\mathbb{E}[V_{(n)}] = \frac{n}{n + 1}$, so multiplying by $\frac{n - 1}{n}$ gives $\frac{n - 1}{n} \cdot \frac{n}{n + 1} = \frac{n - 1}{n + 1}$—identical to Vickrey!

## 2. Revenue Equivalence via the Envelope Theorem

Why wasn't that equality a coincidence? Look how smoothly the **Envelope Theorem** from Lesson 8.1 unlocks the entire mystery in three lines!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Revenue Equivalence Theorem (Vickrey, 1961; Myerson, 1981)**
> Assume $n$ risk-neutral bidders draw independent values from the same smooth distribution on $[0, 1]$, and consider **any** auction design such that (1) the bidder with the highest valuation always wins in equilibrium, and (2) a bidder with zero valuation earns $0$ expected surplus. Then every bidder pays the exact same expected amount and the **seller earns the exact same expected revenue** across all such auctions!

> [!PROOF]
> Let $U(v)$ be the equilibrium expected profit of a bidder whose true valuation is $v$, when choosing the optimal bid $b$:
> $$U(v) = \max_{b} \big(v \cdot P(\text{win} \mid b) - \mathbb{E}[\text{payment} \mid b]\big).$$
> By the **Envelope Theorem** (Lesson 8.1), differentiating $U(v)$ with respect to $v$ simply treats the optimal bid $b^*(v)$ as frozen and differentiates directly with respect to $v$:
> $$U'(v) = P(\text{win at equilibrium} \mid v) = P(\text{all } n - 1 \text{ rivals } < v) = v^{n-1}.$$
> Integrating from $0$ to $v$ with boundary $U(0) = 0$ uniquely nails down every bidder's equilibrium surplus: $U(v) = \frac{v^n}{n}$—completely independent of the payment rules! Since total social value created (always giving the item to the highest value $V_{(n)}$) is identical across formats, subtracting bidders' identical surpluses from total surplus leaves the **exact same expected revenue** for the seller! *(Check: seller revenue $= \mathbb{E}[V_{(n)}] - n \int_0^1 \frac{v^n}{n}\,dv = \frac{n}{n+1} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}$!)*

- **Open-outcry equivalence**: An ascending English auction behaves strategically like a second-price auction (stay in until price hits your true $v$), while a descending Dutch auction behaves like a first-price sealed auction (decide your jump-in cutoff before hearing anyone else!).
- **How can a seller beat $\frac{n-1}{n+1}$? (Myerson's Reserve Price, statement only)**: Rather than letting items sell for pennies when all bids are low, set a **reserve price** $r = 1/2$ (no sale below $1/2$). For independent $\operatorname{Uniform}[0, 1]$ values, a second-price auction with reserve price $1/2$ maximizes expected revenue across *every* possible selling mechanism!

## 3. When Bidders Share a Common Value: The Winner's Curse

What happens when we break the "private independent value" assumption—for example, $n$ oil companies bidding for drilling rights on the **same** tract of land whose true value $V$ is identical for everyone, and each company gets only a noisy geological estimate?

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Winning Can Actually Be Bad News**
> Imagine every bidder forms an unbiased estimate of the true value $V$. Which company actually *wins* the auction? Whichever company got the **most wildly optimistic overestimate**! So if you bid blindly equal to your unconditional estimate without adjusting downward for the fact that beating $n - 1$ rivals means you were the highest draw, winning systematically loses money—a trap famous as the **winner's curse**.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Akerlof's Acquiring-a-Company Puzzle**
> A target company's current owner knows its exact value $V \sim \operatorname{Uniform}[0, 100]$. In your hands, the firm will run $50\%$ more efficiently, making it worth $1.5\, V$ to **you**. How much should you offer?
> Suppose you offer any price $b \in (0, 100]$. The current owner accepts **if and only if** $b \ge V$ (they never sell something worth $\$80$ to them for $\$50$!). Conditional on them saying "yes," $V$ isn't uniform on $[0, 100]$ anymore—it is trapped on $[0, b]$, with expected value $\mathbb{E}[V \mid V \le b] = \frac{b}{2}$! To you, the firm is worth $1.5 \cdot \frac{b}{2} = 0.75\, b$, which is **strictly less than the $b$ you just paid**:
> $$\text{Expected profit} = \frac{b}{100}\left(0.75\, b - b\right) = -\frac{b^2}{400} < 0.$$
> Every single positive offer loses money on average—so the unique rational offer is **$b = 0$**!

## 4. Python Monte Carlo Auction Check

Let us simulate $25{,}000$ independent 4-bidder Uniform$[0, 1]$ auctions across Second-Price, First-Price, and All-Pay rules to verify all three converge to $\frac{4 - 1}{4 + 1} = 0.6000$:

```python
import random

random.seed(7)
n, trials = 4, 25000
rev_second, rev_first, rev_allpay = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
for _ in range(trials):
    vals = sorted(random.random() for _ in range(n))
    rev_second += vals[-2]                        # second-highest bid
    rev_first += ((n - 1) / n) * vals[-1]         # highest first-price bid
    rev_allpay += sum(((n - 1) / n) * (v ** n) for v in vals)

print(f"Theoretical (n-1)/(n+1) = {(n-1)/(n+1):.4f}")
print(f"Simulated Second-Price = {rev_second/trials:.4f}")
print(f"Simulated First-Price  = {rev_first/trials:.4f}")
print(f"Simulated All-Pay      = {rev_allpay/trials:.4f}")
```

```output
Theoretical (n-1)/(n+1) = 0.6000
Simulated Second-Price = 0.5998
Simulated First-Price  = 0.5994
Simulated All-Pay      = 0.5973
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Remember the Fine Print on Revenue Equivalence!**
> Revenue equivalence requires independent private valuations, symmetric risk-neutral bidders, and giving the item to the highest value with $U(0) = 0$. When bidders are risk-averse (preferring first-price shading less!), asymmetric, or chasing a **common value** (causing the winner's curse), different formats no longer yield equal revenue!

## Exercises

1. In a first-price sealed-bid auction with $n = 5$ independent $\operatorname{Uniform}[0, 1]$ bidders, if your private value is $v = 0.80$, how much should you bid in symmetric equilibrium, and what is the seller's expected revenue?
2. Verify by direct integration that with $n = 2$ bidders in an all-pay auction (where equilibrium bid is $b(v) = \frac{1}{2}v^2$), the seller's expected total revenue $2\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{2}V^2]$ equals $\frac{2 - 1}{2 + 1} = \frac{1}{3}$.

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. Optimal bid $b(0.80) = \frac{5 - 1}{5} \times 0.80 = 0.8 \times 0.80 = 0.64$. Expected seller revenue is $\frac{5 - 1}{5 + 1} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.
2. Each of the $2$ bidders pays $b(V_i) = \frac{1}{2} V_i^2$, so expected payment per bidder is $\frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 v^2\,dv = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$. Adding both bidders gives $2 \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$—exact match!
