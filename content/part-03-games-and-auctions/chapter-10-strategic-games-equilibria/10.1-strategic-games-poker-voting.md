Think about playing Rock–Paper–Scissors against a sharp opponent. If you always throw Rock, your opponent switches to Paper and beats you every round. If you throw Rock 80% of the time, they still crush you with Paper! The *only* unexploitable way to play is to randomize equally—$(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$—so that no matter what your opponent throws, they get zero edge. Notice something counterintuitive about that calculation: **you picked your random mix to make your opponent indifferent, not yourself!**

Whenever players move simultaneously or hold hidden cards, simple look-ahead trees fail, and we analyze games through **Nash equilibria**, **von Neumann's Minimax theorem**, **poker bluffing fractions**, and **weighted voting power indices**.

## 1. Strategic Games & The Indifference Principle

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Dominance, Best Response & Nash Equilibrium**
> A **normal-form game** specifies the players, each player's menu of moves (**strategies**), and the payoffs at every combination of moves.
> - Strategy $s$ **strictly dominates** $t$ if $s$ earns strictly more than $t$ against *every* possible choice the opponents could make. Since no rational player chooses $t$, we cross strictly dominated strategies off the table right away.
> - A **best response** maximizes a player's expected payoff given what the others are playing. A **Nash equilibrium** is a list of strategies where *every* player is simultaneously playing a best response—so nobody wishes to deviate alone. *(Nash's Theorem, statement only: every finite game has at least one mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium.)*

> [!INTUITION]
> **The Indifference Principle (How Mixed Equilibria Actually Compute)**
> Why do beginners get stuck computing mixed equilibria? They try setting *their own* strategy payoffs equal—Wait: why should *choosing* between Row 1 and Row 2 depend on my own probabilities $p$? It depends on my *opponent's* probabilities $q$!
> Think about it: if Row mixes Row 1 and Row 2 with probabilities $p$ and $1-p$ in equilibrium, Row must get the exact same expected payoff from Row 1 and Row 2 (otherwise Row would play the higher-paying row 100% of the time!). And **which probabilities make Row indifferent?** The opponent's probabilities $q$! Symmetrically, **you** choose your $p$ to make the **opponent** indifferent between Column 1 and Column 2!

## 2. Two-Player Zero-Sum Games & Closed-Form $2 \times 2$ Formula

In a **zero-sum game** (one player's gain equals the other's loss), put Row's payoffs into a matrix $A$: Row wants to **maximize** $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$ while Column tries to **minimize** it.

> [!THEOREM]
> **Von Neumann's Minimax Theorem**
> For any finite two-player zero-sum game with payoff matrix $A$:
> $$\max_x \min_y x^{\mathsf{T}} A y = \min_y \max_x x^{\mathsf{T}} A y = v,$$
> where $v$ is the unique **value** of the game: Row can guarantee at least $v$, and Column can hold Row to at most $v$.

First check whether the matrix has a **saddle point**—an entry that is simultaneously the smallest number in its row and the largest in its column! If so, players settle into that pure entry immediately without randomizing. Otherwise, for a $2 \times 2$ game $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with no saddle point, equating opponent column payoffs $ap + c(1-p) = bp + d(1-p)$ and row payoffs $aq + b(1-q) = cq + d(1-q)$ yields the clean formulas:

$$p = \frac{d - c}{a - b - c + d}, \qquad q = \frac{d - b}{a - b - c + d}, \qquad v = \frac{ad - bc}{a - b - c + d}.$$

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Solving a $2 \times 2$ Zero-Sum Game Step by Step**
> Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Check row minima: $-1$ (row 1) and $-2$ (row 2). Check column maxima: $3$ (col 1) and $1$ (col 2). Since max-of-row-mins ($-1$) $<$ min-of-col-maxes ($1$), no saddle point exists!
> Set Column's two expected payments equal: $3p + (-2)(1-p) = (-1)p + 1(1-p) \implies 5p - 2 = -2p + 1 \implies 7p = 3 \implies p = \frac{3}{7}$. Setting Row's rows equal gives $3q - 1(1-q) = -2q + 1(1-q) \implies 4q - 1 = -3q + 1 \implies q = \frac{2}{7}$. Checking Row's expected payoff gives $v = 3\left(\frac{2}{7}\right) - 1\left(\frac{5}{7}\right) = \frac{1}{7}$, matching $\frac{ad - bc}{a - b - c + d} = \frac{3 - 2}{3 - (-1) - (-2) + 1} = \frac{1}{7}$!

## 3. Cyclic Dominance & Hidden-Card Poker Betting

Rock–Paper–Scissors generalizes cleanly to any odd cycle $\mathbb{Z}_{2k+1}$, where move $i$ beats the next $k$ moves $i+1, \dots, i+k$ and loses to the previous $k$ moves: by symmetry, uniform play with probability $\frac{1}{2k+1}$ on every move is a Nash equilibrium with value $0$.

Even better, watch how the **Indifference Principle** unlocks real poker strategy in three lines:
- Suppose there is $P$ in the pot, and Player 1 knows whether their hand is a sure winner (*value hand*) or sure loser (*bluff*) and may bet size $S$. Player 2 only holds a bluff-catcher and must decide to Call or Fold.
- **How often should Player 1 bluff? (Pot Odds)**: If Player 1 bets $S$, Player 2 risks calling $S$ against a pot of size $P + S$ (if Player 1 was bluffing, Player 2 wins $P + S$; if Player 1 had a value hand, Player 2 loses $S$). Let $\alpha$ be the fraction of Player 1's bets that are bluffs. To make Player 2 indifferent between Calling (EV $= \alpha(P + S) - (1 - \alpha)S = \alpha(P + 2S) - S$) and Folding (EV $= 0$), we need:
  $$\alpha = \frac{S}{P + 2S}.$$
- **How often must Player 2 defend? (Minimum Defense Frequency)**: Conversely, to stop Player 1 from minting free money by bluffing every dead hand, Player 2 must call with probability $c$ so that a bluff's EV ($c(-S) + (1-c)P$) equals folding ($0$), giving:
  $$c = \frac{P}{P + S}.$$
  If value hands occur with prior frequency $v$, Player 1 bets $100\%$ of value hands and bluffs a fraction $\frac{Sv}{(P+S)(1-v)}$ (capped at $1$) of losing hands! Quick sanity check: for a **pot-sized bet** ($S = P$), $\alpha = \frac{P}{3P} = \frac{1}{3}$ bluffs to $2/3$ value (classical $2:1$ ratio!), and Player 2 defends $c = \frac{P}{2P} = \frac{1}{2}$ of the time!

## 4. Weighted Voting Power: Shapley–Shubik vs Banzhaf

Suppose a board of three shareholders holds voting weights `[2, 1, 1]` and passing a resolution requires quota $q = 3$ votes (written `[3; 2, 1, 1]`). Even though shareholder A only has twice as many shares as B or C (`50%` vs `25%`), how much *actual voting power* does A hold? Two famous indices measure power in different ways:

1. **Shapley–Shubik Index (Shapley Value)**: Line all $n$ voters up in every possible arrival order ($n!$ orders). As voters walk into the room one by one adding their votes, call a voter **pivotal** if their arrival crosses the threshold from $< q$ to $\ge q$. Your power is the fraction of all $n!$ arrival orders in which you are pivotal! *(Equivalently, it is the unique payoff division satisfying Efficiency, Symmetry, Additivity, and Zero for Null players.)*
2. **Banzhaf Index**: List every winning coalition $S$ ($\sum_{i \in S} w_i \ge q$). A voter $i \in S$ is **critical** (a swing voter) if leaving $S$ flips the coalition from passing to failing! Count each voter's critical swings across all coalitions, then normalize by the sum of all players' swings.

Let us compute **both** indices on `[3; 2, 1, 1]` by hand:
- **Shapley–Shubik**: Out of $3! = 6$ orders: `(A, B, C)` $\to$ B pivots; `(A, C, B)` $\to$ C pivots; while `(B, A, C), (C, A, B), (B, C, A), (C, B, A)` all have A crossing $q = 3$ ($4$ orders!). So power shares are `(4/6, 1/6, 1/6) = (2/3, 1/6, 1/6)`!
- **Banzhaf**: Winning coalitions are `{A, B}`, `{A, C}`, and `{A, B, C}`. Voter A is critical in all $3$; B is critical in `{A, B}` ($1$ time); C is critical in `{A, C}` ($1$ time). Total swings $= 3 + 1 + 1 = 5$, giving `(3/5, 1/5, 1/5)`!

## 5. Python Solver for $2 \times 2$ Games & Voting Indices

Let us run Python to verify both our $2 \times 2$ equilibrium solver and the exact Shapley–Shubik / Banzhaf counts for `[3; 2, 1, 1]`:

```python
from itertools import permutations, combinations

# 1. 2x2 zero-sum solver
a, b, c, d = 3, -1, -2, 1
den = a - b - c + d
print(f"2x2 equilibrium: p={(d-c)/den:.4f} (3/7={3/7:.4f}), q={(d-b)/den:.4f} (2/7={2/7:.4f}), v={(a*d-b*c)/den:.4f}")

# 2. Voting power for [3; 2, 1, 1]
weights = {'A': 2, 'B': 1, 'C': 1}
q = 3
pivotal = {'A': 0, 'B': 0, 'C': 0}
for perm in permutations(weights):
    tot = 0
    for p in perm:
        tot += weights[p]
        if tot >= q:
            pivotal[p] += 1
            break
print("Shapley-Shubik [3;2,1,1]:", {k: f"{v}/6" for k, v in pivotal.items()})

critical = {'A': 0, 'B': 0, 'C': 0}
for r in range(1, 4):
    for coal in combinations(weights, r):
        s = sum(weights[p] for p in coal)
        if s >= q:
            for p in coal:
                if s - weights[p] < q:
                    critical[p] += 1
tot_crit = sum(critical.values())
print("Banzhaf [3;2,1,1]:", {k: f"{v}/{tot_crit}" for k, v in critical.items()})
```

```output
2x2 equilibrium: p=0.4286 (3/7=0.4286), q=0.2857 (2/7=0.2857), v=0.1429
Shapley-Shubik [3;2,1,1]: {'A': '4/6', 'B': '1/6', 'C': '1/6'}
Banzhaf [3;2,1,1]: {'A': '3/5', 'B': '1/5', 'C': '1/5'}
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Equate Your Opponent's Payoffs, Never Your Own!**
> Your mixing weights $p$ make the *other player* indifferent across their columns, while the *other player's* weights $q$ make you indifferent across your rows. Mixing up $p$ and $q$ is the #1 exam error on $2 \times 2$ matrix games!

> [!TRAP]
> **Shapley–Shubik vs Banzhaf Count Different Objects**
> Shapley–Shubik counts arrival **permutations** ($n!$), while Banzhaf counts winning **coalitions** ($2^n$ subsets)—so their fractions rarely match and can even swap who looks stronger!

2. Compute both the Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf power indices of the weighted voting game `[4; 3, 2, 1]`. (Hint: does Voter C with weight `1` have *any* power?)

<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. Optimal bluff fraction $\alpha = \frac{S}{P + 2S} = \frac{200}{100 + 400} = \frac{2}{5} = 40\%$ bluffs (approaching $50\%$ for giant overbets!). Minimum defense frequency $c = \frac{P}{P + S} = \frac{100}{300} = \frac{1}{3}$.
2. Look at all winning subsets hitting quota $4$: `{3, 2}`, `{3, 1}`, `{3, 2, 1}`! Wait: adding `{2, 1}` only sums to $3 < 4$! So **Voter C (weight 1)** IS critical in `{3, 1}` ($3+1=4$, drop C leaves $3 < 4$)—C is *not* a dummy! Check Shapley–Shubik across $6$ orders: `(A,B,C)->B`, `(A,C,B)->C`, `(B,A,C)->A`, `(C,A,B)->A`, `(B,C,A)->A` ($2+1=3 < 4$, A brings it to $6$!), `(C,B,A)->A`, giving `(4/6, 1/6, 1/6)`! Check Banzhaf critical swings: `{A,B}` (A,B critical), `{A,C}` (A,C critical), `{A,B,C}` ($6-3=3<4$ so **only** A critical!), giving A: $3$, B: $1$, C: $1$ $\implies$ `(3/5, 1/5, 1/5)`! Even with weights `3, 2, 1`, B and C have identical real voting power!
