In standard chess-like puzzles or coin-removal games, piles keep shrinking, so the game *must* eventually hit an end where someone cannot move. What happens if pieces can move around a **loop** so the same board positions repeat forever—or if every turn along a circle earns points, so neither player ever stops?

Now a third outcome enters alongside **Win (W)** and **Loss (L)**: an infinite **Draw (D)**! To solve these cyclic games without getting stuck in circular logic, we learn three crystal-clear ideas: bottom-up **retrograde analysis** for loopy W/L/D classification, **cycle-breaking mirroring** for circular Nim-style games, and **positional cycle-mean optimization** for long-run payoff graphs.

## 1. Quick Recap: Normal Play & Sprague–Grundy (When Cycles Don't Exist)

When no directed cycles exist (play strictly finishes), every position falls neatly into **N** (*Next player wins*, because there exists a move to a P-position) or **P** (*Previous player wins*, because *every* legal move drops the next player into an N-position). Under **Sprague–Grundy**, playing multiple independent games at once assigns each position an integer **Grundy value** $g(x) = \operatorname{mex}\{g(y) : x \to y\}$ (smallest nonnegative integer absent from its options), and the first player loses a combined sum of games iff the bitwise **XOR** of all component Grundy values is $0$.

## 2. Loopy Games & Retrograde Fixed-Point Labeling

In a **loopy game**, directed cycles allow infinite play, which counts as a **Draw (D)** (neither player forces a win). You cannot start from the root because arrows loop! Instead, start from the dead ends and ripple **backwards** (**retrograde analysis**):

1. Mark every dead-end vertex (zero outgoing arrows) as **L** (if it is your turn here, you lose immediately!).
2. Repeat until an entire pass produces zero new labels: mark any unlabeled position with **at least one** arrow pointing to an **L** as **W** (take that arrow and hand your opponent a loss!), and mark any unlabeled position whose outgoing arrows **all** point to **W** as **L** (every move hands your opponent a winning move!).
3. When propagation halts, **every still-unlabeled position is D (draw)**!

> [!PROOF]
> **Why Unlabeled Vertices Are Exactly the True Draws**
> Notice every **W** or **L** label receives its tag at some finite propagation round $k$, guaranteeing a forced win or forced loss in at most $k$ moves (since round numbers decrease strictly along optimal replies). Now stand on any remaining vertex labeled **D**:
> - Can you accidentally step into a terminal loss right away, or lose? Look—**no** arrow from a **D** vertex points to an **L** (otherwise that **D** vertex would have turned into **W** in Step 2!).
> - Furthermore, **not every** arrow from **D** points to **W** (otherwise it would have turned into **L**!). So at every single **D** position, the moving player *always* has at least one legal escape arrow leading to another **D** position! Both players can keep choosing **D**-staying moves forever, so neither can force a win: the game is genuinely a **Draw**.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Walking Through a 5-Node Loopy Graph**
> Positions `a, b, c, d, e` have moves `a -> b, c`; `b -> a, d`; `c -> e`; `e -> c`; and `d` has no moves.
> - **Round 0**: `d` has zero outgoing moves $\implies$ `d = L`.
> - **Round 1**: `b` sees arrow `b -> d (L)` $\implies$ `b = W`.
> - **Propagation stalls**: What about `a`? It sees `b (W)` and `c (unlabeled)`. Because `c` isn't `L`, `a` doesn't become `W`; because `c` isn't `W`, `a` doesn't become `L`! Meanwhile `c <-> e` loop endlessly. So `a, c, e` all become **D**! Notice the survival tip: starting at `a`, moving to `b` loses immediately (`b -> d`), so Player 1 secures the draw *only* by stepping to `c`!

## 3. Cycle Breaking by Mirroring

Imagine $n$ coins arranged evenly around a circle. On your turn, take either $1$ coin or $2$ *adjacent* coins; whoever takes the last coin wins.

> [!INTUITION]
> **One Move Turns a Circle Into a Line—And Lets Player 2 Copy Everything Forever!**
> Think about what Player 1's very first move does: pulling $1$ or $2$ adjacent coins out of a ring *cuts the ring open* into a straight open line of $n - 1$ or $n - 2$ coins! How can Player 2 immediately force a win for any $n \ge 3$? Remove the exact **middle** $1$ coin (if the open line has odd length) or **middle $2$ coins** (if the open line has even length) so what remains splits into **two completely disconnected equal-length rows**! From then on, every time Player 1 touches a coin in row A, Player 2 mirrors the exact same move in row B—always matching Player 1 and taking the very last coin! *(Only $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ give Player 1 a win by taking the whole circle at move 1.)*

When rules aren't symmetric enough for mirroring, compute `g(cycle)` just as an open path game: `g(cycle) = mex{ g(path) }` over every possible first removal!

## 4. Mean-Payoff (Cyclic) Games: Finding Optimal Cycle Averages

Now place real dollar rewards (negative or positive) on every directed edge of a finite graph where every vertex belongs to either **Max** (who moves next to push the long-run average edge reward up) or **Min** (who tries to pull it down).

- **Ehrenfeucht–Mycielski Theorem (1979, statement only)**: Remarkably, every starting vertex has a well-defined game value, and **neither player ever needs memory or randomization**—both possess optimal **positional** strategies (picking a fixed favorite exit arrow at each of their vertices forever!).
- **Equivalence to Finite First-Cycle Stopping**: Suppose players follow fixed exit arrows until the token visits *any* vertex for the second time, closing a single directed loop, and then stop immediately with payoff equal to the average weight around that closed loop! Gurvich, Karzanov, and Khachiyan (1988) proved both the infinite and finite loop-closing games have the **exact same value**!

### Concrete Calculation on a 3-Vertex Graph

Let vertices `1, 3` belong to **Max**, vertex `2` belong to **Min**, with weighted edges: `1 -> 2 (4)`, `1 -> 3 (1)`, `2 -> 1 (0)`, `2 -> 3 (2)`, `3 -> 1 (1)`, `3 -> 2 (-3)`. Starting at vertex `1`, Max has $2 \times 2 = 4$ positional arrow choices:

| Max's Positional Choice | Min's Best Reply | Closed Cycle | Mean Edge Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1 -> 2, 3 -> 1` | `2 -> 1` | `1 -> 2 -> 1` | $(4 + 0)/2 = \mathbf{2}$ |
| `1 -> 2, 3 -> 2` | `2 -> 3` | `2 -> 3 -> 2` | $(2 - 3)/2 = -1/2$ |
| `1 -> 3, 3 -> 1` | any (`2` unreachable) | `1 -> 3 -> 1` | $(1 + 1)/2 = 1$ |
| `1 -> 3, 3 -> 2` | `2 -> 1` | `1 -> 3 -> 2 -> 1` | $(1 - 3 + 0)/3 = -2/3$ |

Comparing the worst-case rows, Max secures guaranteed mean payoff **`2`** by choosing `1 -> 2` and `3 -> 1`! (Note: if Min tried switching to `2 -> 3`, Max's edges would close `1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 1` with even higher mean $(4 + 2 + 1)/3 = 7/3 > 2$, so Min sticks with `2 -> 1`.)

## 5. Python Retrograde Analyzer

Let us test our loopy `W / L / D` retrograde fixed-point algorithm on the 5-node graph `a..e` in Python:

```python
def retrograde(nodes, edges):
    status = {}
    for u in nodes:
        if len(edges[u]) == 0:
            status[u] = 'L'
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for u in nodes:
            if u in status:
                continue
            succ = edges[u]
            if any(status.get(v) == 'L' for v in succ):
                status[u] = 'W'
                changed = True
            elif succ and all(status.get(v) == 'W' for v in succ):
                status[u] = 'L'
                changed = True
    for u in nodes:
        status.setdefault(u, 'D')
    return status

nodes = ['a', 'b', 'c', 'd', 'e']
edges = {'a': ['b', 'c'], 'b': ['a', 'd'], 'c': ['e'], 'd': [], 'e': ['c']}
print("Retrograde W/L/D labels:", retrograde(nodes, edges))
```

```output
Retrograde W/L/D labels: {'d': 'L', 'b': 'W', 'a': 'D', 'c': 'D', 'e': 'D'}
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Avoidance of Loss Does Not Equal a Win!**
> In loopy games, trapping play in a perpetual loop is classified strictly as **`D` (Draw)**, never `W`! Always inspect unlabeled vertices after stabilization rather than assuming every non-loss is a win.

> [!TRAP]
> **Check Parity Cases When Mirroring on a Circle**
> Mirroring works only if Player 2 can leave **two completely identical independent halves**. Always verify both odd and even path remainders—and double-check whether any extra rule (like "pieces must stay adjacent") accidentally breaks mirroring!
