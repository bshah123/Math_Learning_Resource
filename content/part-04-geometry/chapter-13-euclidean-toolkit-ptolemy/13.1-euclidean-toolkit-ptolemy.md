Look closely at any Olympiad or Math Bowl geometry diagram—circles crossing triangles, cevians meeting at a point, lengths multiplied together. Rather than groping blindly in coordinates, experienced problem solvers rely on four clean engines: **triangle metric identities**, **circle angle-chasing**, **power of a point**, and **Ptolemy's theorem**.

In this lesson we organize those four tools cleanly from definitions to concrete triangle examples, then give **two** completely different proofs of Ptolemy's Theorem (one by complex numbers and one by classical similar triangles!) and watch how five famous trig and polygon formulas drop right out.

## 1. The Triangle Metric & Center Toolkit

Label $\triangle ABC$ in standard convention: opposite sides $a = BC$, $b = CA$, $c = AB$, semiperimeter $s = \frac{a + b + c}{2}$, area $K$, circumradius $R$ (outer circle), and inradius $r$ (inner tangent circle).

- **Sines, Cosines & Area**: $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$ (never forget the factor of $2$!); $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C$; and four faces of triangle area:
  $$K = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C = rs = \frac{abc}{4R} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)} \quad (\text{Heron}).$$
- **Four Classical Centers & Euler's Line**: The **Centroid $G$** cuts every median in a $2:1$ ratio; the **Circumcenter $O$** (perpendicular bisectors, outer circle), **Incenter $I$** (angle bisectors, inner circle), and **Orthocenter $H$** (altitudes) meet their respective lines—and remarkably, $O, G, H$ **always lie on one straight line** (the **Euler line**), with $OH = 3 \cdot OG$.
- **Euler's Distance Formula ($OI$)**: Comparing circumcircle and incircle centers gives $OI^2 = R(R - 2r)$—forcing **$R \ge 2r$** immediately (since $OI^2 \ge 0$), with equality iff $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral!
- **Angle Bisector & Stewart's Theorem**: An angle bisector from $A$ cuts opposite side $BC$ at $D$ in the side ratio $BD/DC = c/b$. For *any* cevian $AD$ of length $d$ splitting $BC$ into segments $BD = m$ and $DC = n$ ($m + n = a$), **Stewart's Theorem** states:
  $$b^2 m + c^2 n = a(d^2 + mn).$$
  *(Memory mnemonic: "A man and his dad put a bomb in the sink": $man + dad = bmb + cnc$!)*
- **Ceva & Menelaus**: Lines $AD, BE, CF$ from vertices to opposite sides meet at one point iff $\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB} = 1$ (**Ceva**). Three points $D, E, F$ on extensions of sides $BC, CA, AB$ lie on one straight line iff the same product of *signed* segment ratios equals $-1$ (**Menelaus**).

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Testing Everything on the Famous 13–14–15 Triangle**
> With $(a, b, c) = (13, 14, 15)$, semiperimeter $s = (13 + 14 + 15)/2 = 21$.
> Heron gives $K = \sqrt{21(21 - 13)(21 - 14)(21 - 15)} = \sqrt{21 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6} = 84$.
> Inradius is $r = \frac{K}{s} = \frac{84}{21} = 4$, and circumradius is $R = \frac{13 \cdot 14 \cdot 15}{4 \cdot 84} = \frac{65}{8} = 8.125$.
> Finally Euler's formula gives $OI^2 = \frac{65}{8}\left(\frac{65}{8} - 8\right) = \frac{65}{64}$, so the distance between $O$ and $I$ is $\frac{\sqrt{65}}{8} \approx 1.0078$!

## 2. Circles, Cyclic Quads & Power of a Point

Angle-chasing inside circles relies on four short rules:
- **Inscribed angle**: An inscribed angle measures half its central angle (so any triangle with diameter as a side is right-angled: Thales's theorem!), and two inscribed angles standing on the same arc are equal.
- **Cyclic quadrilaterals**: Four vertices $A, B, C, D$ (in cyclic perimeter order) sit on a common circle iff opposite angles sum to $180^\circ$ ($\angle A + \angle C = 180^\circ$), equivalently iff diagonal-viewed side angles tie ($\angle ABD = \angle ACD$).
- **Tangent–chord rule**: The angle squeezed between a tangent and a chord equals the inscribed angle standing on that chord on the far side; two tangents drawn from an outside point $P$ have equal length.
- **Power of a Point & Radical Axis**: Fix a circle centered at $O$ of radius $\rho$, and pick any point $P$. Every straight line through $P$ slicing the circle at $X$ and $Y$ gives the *exact same constant* product:
  $$PX \cdot PY = |OP^2 - \rho^2|,$$
  and if $P$ lies outside with tangent segment $PT$, then $PT^2 = OP^2 - \rho^2$! Comparing two circles, points having equal power to both form a straight line perpendicular to their line of centers (the **radical axis**), and the three pairwise radical axes of three circles meet at a single point (**radical center**)!

## 3. Ptolemy's Theorem & Inequality

When do four points lie on one circle in terms of their **six pairwise distances** alone? Claudius Ptolemy answered this around 150 AD, and his identity remains one of the sharpest tools in Olympiad geometry.

> [!THEOREM]
> **Ptolemy's Theorem & General Inequality**
> For any four points $A, B, C, D$ in the plane:
> $$AC \cdot BD \le AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC,$$
> with exact equality ($AC \cdot BD = AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC$, "product of diagonals = sum of products of opposite sides") **if and only if** $A, B, C, D$ lie on one circle (or straight line) in that cyclic order!

> [!PROOF]
> **Two Complementary Proofs**
> 1. **Complex-Number Proof (handles general inequality + iff at once!)**: Place $A, B, C, D$ in the complex plane at $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{C}$. Expand the right-hand side of the elementary algebraic identity:
> $$(a - c)(b - d) = (a - b)(c - d) + (a - d)(b - c).$$
> Check: $(ac - ad - bc + bd) + (ab - ac - bd + cd) = ab + cd - ad - bc = (a-c)(b-d)$—Wait: expand $(a-b)(c-d) = ac - ad - bc + bd$ and $(a-d)(b-c) = ab - ac - bd + cd$; adding gives $ab - ad - bc + cd = (a-c)(b-d)$! Now take absolute values (`|z1 + z2| <= |z1| + |z2|`!) to get $AC \cdot BD \le AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC$ immediately! Equality in the triangle inequality holds iff the ratio of the two right-hand complex numbers $\frac{(a-b)(c-d)}{(a-d)(b-c)}$ is a positive real number—meaning its argument $\arg\frac{a-b}{a-d} + \arg\frac{c-d}{b-c} \equiv 0 \pmod{2\pi}$, which says opposite angles $\angle DAB$ and $\angle BCD$ sum to $180^\circ$ (concyclic in order $A, B, C, D$)!
> 2. **Classical Similar-Triangles Proof (cyclic case)**: Pick point $E$ along diagonal $BD$ so that $\angle BAE = \angle CAD$. Because $A, B, C, D$ lie on a circle, $\angle ABD = \angle ACD$, giving $\triangle ABE \sim \triangle ACD \implies \frac{AB}{AC} = \frac{BE}{CD} \implies AB \cdot CD = AC \cdot BE$. Next note $\angle BAC = \angle EAD$ (adding $\angle EAC$ to both!) and $\angle ACB = \angle ADB$, giving $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle AED \implies \frac{AD}{AC} = \frac{ED}{BC} \implies AD \cdot BC = AC \cdot ED$. Adding both equations yields $AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC = AC(BE + ED) = AC \cdot BD$!

### Five Classic Consequences Pop Right Out

1. **Pythagoras's Theorem**: Apply Ptolemy to a rectangle of sides $a, b$ and equal diagonals $d$: $d \cdot d = a\cdot a + b\cdot b \implies d^2 = a^2 + b^2$.
2. **Golden Ratio in a Regular Pentagon**: Take $4$ consecutive vertices of a regular pentagon (side $s$, equal diagonals $d$). Notice $3$ edges are $s$ and the $2$ diagonals + 4th side complete $d$: $d^2 = d\cdot s + s^2 \implies (d/s)^2 - (d/s) - 1 = 0 \implies d/s = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$.
3. **Regular Heptagon Reciprocal Identity**: For a regular 7-gon with side $a$ and diagonals $b < c$, applying Ptolemy to vertices `1, 2, 3, 5` gives $bc = ab + ac$; dividing by $abc$ gives $\frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c}$!
4. **Sine Addition Formula**: Place quadrilateral $ABCD$ inside a unit-diameter circle ($2R = 1$), with diagonal $AC = 1$ as a diameter, $\angle BAC = \alpha$, and $\angle DAC = \beta$. Then chords give $BC = \sin\alpha$, $CD = \sin\beta$, $AB = \cos\alpha$, $AD = \cos\beta$, and $BD = \sin(\alpha + \beta)$—turning Ptolemy directly into $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$!
5. **Pompeiu's Theorem**: Place point $P$ anywhere relative to equilateral $\triangle ABC$ of side $s$. By Ptolemy's inequality, $PA \cdot s \le PB \cdot s + PC \cdot s \implies PA \le PB + PC$ (so $PA, PB, PC$ always form a triangle!), and **equality $PA = PB + PC$ holds iff $P$ sits right on the minor arc $BC$** of the circumcircle!

## 4. Python Numeric Geometry Check

Let us verify all metrics of the `13-14-15` triangle and the exact heptagon identity $\frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c}$ in Python:

```python
import math

# 1. 13-14-15 triangle metrics
a, b, c = 13, 14, 15
s = (a + b + c) / 2
K = math.sqrt(s * (s - a) * (s - b) * (s - c))
r = K / s
R = (a * b * c) / (4 * K)
OI2 = R * (R - 2 * r)
print(f"13-14-15: K={K}, r={r}, R={R} (65/8={65/8}), OI^2={OI2} (65/64={65/64})")

# 2. Regular heptagon 1/a = 1/b + 1/c in unit circumcircle
a7 = 2 * math.sin(math.pi / 7)
b7 = 2 * math.sin(2 * math.pi / 7)
c7 = 2 * math.sin(3 * math.pi / 7)
print(f"Heptagon check: 1/a={1/a7:.6f}, 1/b + 1/c={1/b7 + 1/c7:.6f}")
```

```output
13-14-15: K=84.0, r=4.0, R=8.125 (65/8=8.125), OI^2=1.015625 (65/64=1.015625)
Heptagon check: 1/a=1.152382, 1/b + 1/c=1.152382
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Cyclic Order Matters in Ptolemy, and Law of Sines Uses $2R$!**
> If you scramble the order of vertices from $A, B, C, D$ around the circle to $A, C, B, D$, you swap which segments are diagonals vs sides—so equality flips to strict inequality! Also always double-check whether a formula wants $R$ ($K = \frac{abc}{4R}$) or diameter $2R$ ($\frac{a}{\sin A} = 2R$).

2. Point $P$ lies on the minor arc $BC$ of the circumcircle of equilateral $\triangle ABC$. If $PB = 3$ and $PC = 5$, find the distance $PA$!

<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. $s = (5 + 6 + 7)/2 = 9$. Heron: $K = \sqrt{9(9-5)(9-6)(9-7)} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2} = 6\sqrt{6} \approx 14.697$. Then $r = \frac{K}{s} = \frac{6\sqrt{6}}{9} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3} \approx 1.633$, and $R = \frac{5\cdot 6\cdot 7}{4(6\sqrt{6})} = \frac{35}{4\sqrt{6}} \approx 3.572$. Notice $R = 3.572 \ge 2r = 3.266$!
2. By **Pompeiu's Theorem** (Ptolemy on cyclic $ABPC$ with $AB = BC = CA = s$), $PA \cdot s = PB \cdot s + PC \cdot s$, so dividing by $s$ gives $PA = PB + PC = 3 + 5 = 8$!
