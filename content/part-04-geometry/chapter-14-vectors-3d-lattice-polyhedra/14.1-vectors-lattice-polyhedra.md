When synthetic angle-chasing hits a 3D wall—planes intersecting in space, skewed distances, integer grid polygons, or polyhedral angles—**vectors** and **Euler-style counting** turn spatial puzzles into clean arithmetic. Dot products test right angles in one multiplication, cross products measure flat areas, Pick's theorem turns area inside integer grids into dot-counting, and Descartes's angular-defect law explains why there can *never* be a sixth Platonic solid!

## 1. Vectors, Planes & 3D Pythagoras (De Gua)

- **Dot product**: $u \cdot v = |u||v|\cos\theta$, immediately giving perpendicularity ($u \perp v \iff u \cdot v = 0$) and orthogonal projection of $u$ onto $v$ as $\frac{u \cdot v}{|v|^2} v$. Squaring a sum gives **Vector Pythagoras**: $|u_1 + \dots + u_k|^2 = \sum |u_i|^2 + 2\sum_{i<j} u_i \cdot u_j$.
- **Cross product (3D only!)**: $u \times v$ stands perpendicular to both $u$ and $v$ with length $|u||v|\sin\theta$ (area of the parallelogram spanned by $u, v$). Thus $\triangle ABC$ has area $\frac{1}{2}|AB \times AC|$.
- **Scalar triple product**: $u \cdot (v \times w) = \det[u;\; v;\; w]$ computes the signed volume of a 3D parallelepiped (and a tetrahedron has $1/6$ of that volume; four points are coplanar iff the determinant is $0$).
- **2D Polygon Areas (Shoelace Formula)**: Walk around any simple polygon $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$ in order:
  $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i)\right| \qquad ((x_{n+1}, y_{n+1}) = (x_1, y_1)).$$
- **Planes and 3D Distances**: The plane through $A, B, C$ has normal $n = (B - A) \times (C - A)$ and equation $n \cdot (X - A) = 0$. Distance from $P(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ to plane $ax + by + cz = d$ is $\frac{|ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0 - d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$, and distance to line $A + t u$ is $\frac{|(P - A) \times u|}{|u|}$. The angle between two planes equals the angle between their normals, while the angle between a line and a plane is $90^\circ$ minus the angle between the line and the normal.
- **De Gua's Theorem (3D Right Triangle)**: Take a corner of a room where three edges meet mutually at $90^\circ$, slice off the corner to form a right tetrahedron $OABC$. Just like Pythagoras $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, the **squared area** of the slanting front face $\triangle ABC$ equals the sum of the squared areas of the three right-angled wall faces:
  $$\operatorname{Area}(\triangle ABC)^2 = \operatorname{Area}(\triangle OAB)^2 + \operatorname{Area}(\triangle OBC)^2 + \operatorname{Area}(\triangle OCA)^2.$$

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Testing De Gua and Plane Distance on $A(1,0,0), B(0,2,0), C(0,0,3)$**
> Vectors $AB = (-1, 2, 0)$ and $AC = (-1, 0, 3)$ have cross product $n = (6, 3, 2)$ of length $\sqrt{36 + 9 + 4} = 7$.
> So plane $ABC$ is $6x + 3y + 2z = 6$, distance from origin $O$ is $\frac{6}{7}$, and $\operatorname{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{7}{2} = 3.5$.
> Check De Gua: the three coordinate right triangles have areas $\frac{1\cdot 2}{2} = 1$, $\frac{2\cdot 3}{2} = 3$, and $\frac{1\cdot 3}{2} = 1.5$. Summing their squares gives $1^2 + 3^2 + 1.5^2 = 1 + 9 + 2.25 = 12.25 = (3.5)^2$!

## 2. Integer Grid Geometry & Pick's Theorem

Draw a simple polygon whose corners all land on integer coordinate grid dots $(x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ (a **lattice polygon**). Instead of chopping it into messy triangles, Georg Pick noticed in 1899 you can simply count grid dots!

- First, along any grid segment joining $(x_1, y_1)$ to $(x_2, y_2)$, how many grid dots lie on it (including both ends)? Exactly $\gcd(|x_2 - x_1|, |y_2 - y_1|) + 1$!
- **Pick's Theorem**: If a simple lattice polygon (no holes!) has $I$ lattice dots strictly inside and $B$ lattice dots along its boundary, its exact area is:
  $$\text{Area} = I + \frac{B}{2} - 1.$$
  *Example*: Check triangle $(0,0), (5,0), (2,4)$. Base $5$, height $4 \implies \text{Area} = 10$. Boundary dots on the three edges: $\gcd(5,0) + \gcd(3,4) + \gcd(2,4) = 5 + 1 + 2 = 8 = B$. Pick's formula predicts $10 = I + \frac{8}{2} - 1 \implies I = 7$ interior grid dots!
- **Why You Can Never Draw an Equilateral Triangle on 2D Graph Paper**: Every lattice polygon in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has area a half-integer (from Shoelace, $\frac{1}{2}\text{integer}$!). Yet an equilateral triangle with integer squared side $s^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ has area $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2$, which is **irrational**! In fact, the **only** regular polygon fitting on a 2D integer grid is a square! *(Plot twist: step into 3D lattice $\mathbb{Z}^3$—then $(1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)$ forms an equilateral triangle of side $\sqrt{2}$!)*

## 3. Convex Polyhedra & Why Exactly Five Platonic Solids Exist

Every convex polyhedron satisfies **Euler's formula** $V - E + F = 2$. At every corner (vertex), flatten out the corner slightly: the face angles meeting around a flat point sum to $360^\circ$, whereas at a sharp convex 3D corner they sum to *strictly less* than $360^\circ$! Call $360^\circ - (\text{sum of face angles at } v)$ the **angular defect** at corner $v$. **Descartes's Theorem** proves that adding the angular defects across *every* vertex of any convex polyhedron always totals exactly $720^\circ$! (Compare with Gauss–Bonnet: a cube has $8$ corners each lacking $360^\circ - 3(90^\circ) = 90^\circ$, giving $8 \times 90^\circ = 720^\circ$!)

Now make every face an identical regular $p$-gon ($p \ge 3$) and require $q \ge 3$ faces to meet at every corner. Because each corner must have positive angular defect (sum of angles $< 360^\circ$) and one interior angle of a regular $p$-gon is $\frac{p-2}{p}180^\circ$:
$$q \cdot \frac{p - 2}{p} 180^\circ < 360^\circ \iff (p - 2)q < 2p \iff pq - 2p - 2q + 4 < 4 \iff (p - 2)(q - 2) < 4.$$
Look at positive integers $p - 2 \ge 1$ and $q - 2 \ge 1$ whose product is below $4$: only **$5$ pairs** exist—proving there are *only five Platonic solids in the universe*!

| Platonic Solid | Face $p$ | Corners Meet $q$ | $V$ | $E$ | $F$ | Dual Solid (Swap $V \leftrightarrow F$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tetrahedron | triangle ($3$) | $3$ | $4$ | $6$ | $4$ | Tetrahedron (self-dual) |
| Cube | square ($4$) | $3$ | $8$ | $12$ | $6$ | Octahedron |
| Octahedron | triangle ($3$) | $4$ | $6$ | $12$ | $8$ | Cube |
| Dodecahedron | pentagon ($5$) | $3$ | $20$ | $30$ | $12$ | Icosahedron |
| Icosahedron | triangle ($3$) | $5$ | $12$ | $30$ | $20$ | Dodecahedron |

Keep the twin edge-$a$ formulas for **tetrahedron** and **octahedron** side by side:

| Metric (Edge Length $a$) | Regular Tetrahedron | Regular Octahedron |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | $a^3 \sqrt{2} / 12$ | $a^3 \sqrt{2} / 3$ ($4\times$ tetrahedron!) |
| Surface area | $a^2 \sqrt{3}$ | $2 a^2 \sqrt{3}$ |
| Circumradius | $a \sqrt{6} / 4$ | $a \sqrt{2} / 2$ |
| Inradius | $a \sqrt{6} / 12$ | $a \sqrt{6} / 6$ |
| Dihedral angle | $\arccos(1/3) \approx 70.53^\circ$ | $\arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47^\circ$ |

Look at those last two numbers: $70.53^\circ + 109.47^\circ = \mathbf{180^\circ}$! Their dihedral angles are supplementary—meaning tetrahedra and octahedra lock cleanly together to tile all of 3D space (explored in Lesson 16.1!).

## 4. Python Verifier

Let us verify the plane distance + De Gua identity and Pick's theorem on `(0,0), (5,0), (2,4)` in Python:

```python
import math

# 1. Verify plane through A(1,0,0), B(0,2,0), C(0,0,3) and De Gua's theorem
def cross(u, v):
    return (u[1]*v[2]-u[2]*v[1], u[2]*v[0]-u[0]*v[2], u[0]*v[1]-u[1]*v[0])

AB, AC = (-1, 2, 0), (-1, 0, 3)
n = cross(AB, AC)
area_abc = math.sqrt(sum(c*c for c in n)) / 2
print("Normal:", n, "Area ABC:", area_abc, "Origin distance:", abs(n[0]*1)/math.sqrt(sum(c*c for c in n)))
print("De Gua sum of squares:", (0.5*1*2)**2 + (0.5*2*3)**2 + (0.5*1*3)**2, "vs Area^2:", area_abc**2)

# 2. Pick's theorem for (0,0), (5,0), (2,4)
pts = [(0, 0), (5, 0), (2, 4)]
area2 = abs(sum(pts[i][0]*pts[(i+1)%3][1] - pts[(i+1)%3][0]*pts[i][1] for i in range(3)))
B = sum(math.gcd(abs(pts[i][0]-pts[(i+1)%3][0]), abs(pts[i][1]-pts[(i+1)%3][1])) for i in range(3))
I = (area2 - B + 2) // 2
print(f"Pick triangle: Area={area2/2}, B={B}, I={I}")
```

```output
Normal: (6, 3, 2) Area ABC: 3.5 Origin distance: 0.8571428571428571
De Gua sum of squares: 12.25 vs Area^2: 12.25
Pick triangle: Area=10.0, B=8, I=7
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Cross Products Live Strictly in 3D, and Pick Forbids Holes**
> Remember $u \times v = -(v \times u)$ is anti-commutative and only defined in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Similarly, Pick's formula $\text{Area} = I + B/2 - 1$ breaks down if a lattice polygon contains internal holes or crosses itself!

## Exercises

1. A regular icosahedron has $F = 20$ triangular faces and $5$ triangles meeting at every vertex. Use Descartes's angular-defect theorem to check $V = 12$ without memorizing the table!
2. Find the area and number of interior lattice points $I$ of the lattice triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(6, 0)$, and $(0, 4)$.

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. At each corner of an icosahedron, $5$ equilateral triangles ($60^\circ$ each) meet, summing to $300^\circ$. Each vertex therefore has angular defect $360^\circ - 300^\circ = 60^\circ$. By Descartes's theorem, all vertex defects sum to $720^\circ$, giving $V = 720^\circ / 60^\circ = 12$ vertices! (Alternatively, each face has $3$ corners shared by $5$ triangles: $V = 20 \times 3 / 5 = 12$.)
2. Area $= \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12$. Boundary dots: along $(0,0)-(6,0)$ is $\gcd(6,0) = 6$ steps; along $(6,0)-(0,4)$ is $\gcd(6,4) = 2$ steps; along $(0,4)-(0,0)$ is $\gcd(0,4) = 4$ steps; total $B = 6 + 2 + 4 = 12$. By Pick's theorem, $12 = I + \frac{12}{2} - 1 \implies I = 7$ interior points!
