Give a mathematician just **three points** (not in a straight line), and look how many rich geometry, surveying, geology, and probability puzzles immediately spring to life:
1. Finding the unique **circle** passing through three points (and testing whether a 4th joins them).
2. Recovering an underground tilted rock layer from three **geologist boreholes**.
3. Locating the optimal warehouse (**Fermat point**) minimizing total cable length $PA + PB + PC$.
4. Determining your own map coordinates (**Snellius–Pothenot resection**) just by measuring two angles toward three known peaks.
5. Computing the exact odds that a triangle formed by **three random dots on a circle** swallows the center!

Let us solve each of these five classic problems with full intuition and zero hand-waving.

## 1. Problem 1: The Circle Through Three Points

Three non-collinear points $A, B, C$ determine a unique circle whose center sits at the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $BC$, with circumradius $R = \frac{abc}{4K}$. In coordinates:
- **Test for collinearity**: Three points $(x_i, y_i)$ ($i=1,2,3$) lie on one line iff the $3 \times 3$ determinant with rows $(x_i, y_i, 1)$ vanishes (zero Shoelace area).
- **Test for concyclicity**: Because any circle equation looks like $c(x^2 + y^2) + dx + ey + f = 0$, a fourth point joins $A, B, C$ on one circle (or one straight line!) iff the $4 \times 4$ determinant with rows $(x_i^2 + y_i^2,\; x_i,\; y_i,\; 1)$ equals $0$—matching Ptolemy's Theorem in Lesson 13.1!

## 2. Problem 2: Geologist's Borehole Problem (Plane Through 3 Points)

Geologists drill three vertical test wells at map locations $(x_i, y_i)$ and hit a tilted flat rock seam at heights $z_i$. Describing the flat seam as $z = px + qy + r$, plugging in the three readings solves a $3 \times 3$ linear system for $(p, q, r)$. What do $(p, q)$ mean visually?
- The vector $(p, q)$ points straight **uphill** (steepest rise on the map), so the seam's **dip angle** $\delta$ (steepest tilt from horizontal) obeys:
  $$\tan\delta = \sqrt{p^2 + q^2}.$$
- Walking perpendicular to $(p, q)$ stays completely flat at constant elevation—defining the seam's horizontal **strike** direction!
*Quick check*: Boreholes reading $10$ at $(0,0)$, $0$ at $(100,0)$, and $5$ at $(0,50)$ immediately give $r = 10$, $100p + 10 = 0 \implies p = -0.1$, and $50q + 10 = 5 \implies q = -0.1$. So $\tan\delta = \sqrt{(-0.1)^2 + (-0.1)^2} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{10}$, giving a gentle $\delta \approx 8.05^\circ$ dip!

## 3. Problem 3: The Fermat Point (Minimizing $PA + PB + PC$)

Suppose three towns $A, B, C$ need a single power hub $P$ minimizing total cable length $PA + PB + PC$. Calculus gets tangled in square roots—so Pierre de Fermat and Evangelista Torricelli solved it using a simple **$60^\circ$ rotation**!

- **Case 1 (All angles of $\triangle ABC$ are $< 120^\circ$)**: The minimum occurs at the unique interior **Fermat point** $F$ seeing all three sides at equal $120^\circ$ angles ($\angle AFB = \angle BFC = \angle CFA = 120^\circ$).
- **Case 2 (Some angle, say $\angle A \ge 120^\circ$)**: Rotating forces the line outside the triangle—the true minimum collapses straight onto **that obtuse vertex $A$ itself** (total cable $AB + AC$)!

> [!PROOF]
> **Why a $60^\circ$ Rotation Unfolds the Sum Into One Straight Line**
> Pick any candidate hub $P$, and rotate the entire $\triangle APB$ rigidly by $60^\circ$ around $B$ outward away from $C$, landing at $\triangle A'P'B$. Because $BP$ rotated $60^\circ$ to $BP'$ with equal length, $\triangle BPP'$ is **equilateral**, meaning the dashed middle step $P'P$ equals $PB$! Meanwhile rotation kept $PA = A'P'$. Look at our total sum now:
> $$PA + PB + PC = A'P' + P'P + PC.$$
> That is literally the length of a **three-segment zigzag path** from the fixed rotated corner $A'$ through $P'$ and $P$ to the fixed corner $C$! By the Triangle Inequality, the shortest path between $A'$ and $C$ is the straight line segment $A'C$! When can the zigzag flatten straight along $A'C$? Precisely when $\angle A'P'P = 180^\circ$ and $\angle P'PC = 180^\circ$—and since the equilateral triangle contributes $60^\circ$, that forces $\angle APB = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$ and $\angle BPC = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$!

![Fermat Point 60-Degree Rotation Proof](diagrams/15.1-fermat-point.svg "Rotating triangle APB by 60 degrees unfolds PA + PB + PC into A'P' + P'P + PC >= A'C")

- **Geometric construction & exact length**: Build outward equilateral triangles on all three sides; joining each outer peak to the opposite vertex gives three lines meeting at $F$, each of exact minimal length! When all angles are $< 120^\circ$, squaring $A'C$ via the Law of Cosines gives:
  $$(PA + PB + PC)_{\min}^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}{2} + 2\sqrt{3}\,K.$$
  On the `3-4-5` right triangle ($K = 6$, largest angle $90^\circ < 120^\circ$), the shortest total cable is $\sqrt{(9+16+25)/2 + 12\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{25 + 12\sqrt{3}} \approx 6.7664$ (much shorter than picking the right-angle corner $3 + 4 = 7$!).

## 4. Problem 4: Resection (Where Am I? — Snellius–Pothenot)

Imagine standing at sea or in open desert at an unknown point $P$, spotting three mapped mountain peaks $A, B, C$, and measuring only two angles with a compass/sextant: $\alpha = \angle APB$ and $\beta = \angle BPC$. All points seeing segment $AB$ at fixed inscribed angle $\alpha$ trace out a circle through $A$ and $B$ (centered on $AB$'s bisector, radius $\frac{AB}{2\sin\alpha}$). Similarly, points seeing $BC$ at $\beta$ trace a second circle through $B$ and $C$. Since both circles already cross at landmark $B$, their *second* intersection nails down your position $P$!

**Watch out for the Danger Circle!** What if you happen to stand on the circumcircle passing through all three peaks $A, B, C$? Then by the inscribed angle theorem, *every single point* along that arc sees $AB$ at $\alpha$ and $BC$ at $\beta$—both arcs collapse onto the exact same circle, leaving your location completely undetermined!

## 5. Problem 5: Three Random Points on a Circle

Drop three dots independently and uniformly at random around a circle, and connect them into an inscribed triangle. What is the probability that the triangle **contains the center of the circle** (equivalently, is an **acute** triangle)? Amazingly, the answer is a clean **$1/4$**!

> [!PROOF]
> **Why Mutually Exclusive Semicircles Solve $n$ Points at Once**
> A triangle inscribed in a circle **misses** the center if and only if all three vertices sit huddled inside *some* open half-circle (semicircle)! Generalize to $n$ random dots $X_1, \dots, X_n$ on the circle:
> Wait—whenever all $n$ dots lie inside a single semicircle, trace that semicircle clockwise: there is a **unique** "clockwise-starting dot" $X_i$ such that all $n - 1$ remaining dots lie inside the $180^\circ$ clockwise arc right after $X_i$!
> How likely is it for a given dot $X_i$ that all other $n - 1$ dots land inside the $180^\circ$ arc after it? Half a circle has probability $1/2$ per dot, so $2^{-(n-1)}$! Better yet, because *at most one* dot can be the clockwise leader of an occupied half-circle (unless two points hit the exact diameter, probability $0$), these $n$ events are **completely mutually exclusive**! Adding their probabilities gives:
> $$P(\text{center missed}) = \frac{n}{2^{n-1}}, \qquad P(\text{center inside}) = 1 - \frac{n}{2^{n-1}}.$$
> Plug in $n = 3$ dots: missing the center has probability $3 / 2^2 = 3/4$, leaving **$1 - 3/4 = 1/4$**!

## 6. Python Three-Point Solver

Let us verify the borehole dip angle and the `3-4-5` triangle's exact Fermat sum in Python:

```python
import math

# 1. Borehole dip angle: z = px + qy + r at (0,0)->10, (100,0)->0, (0,50)->5
r = 10.0
p = (0.0 - r) / 100.0
q = (5.0 - r) / 50.0
dip_deg = math.degrees(math.atan(math.hypot(p, q)))
print(f"Geologist plane: p={p}, q={q}, tan(delta)={math.hypot(p,q):.6f}, dip={dip_deg:.2f} deg")

# 2. Fermat point minimal sum for 3-4-5 triangle vs 120-deg-plus obtuse triangle
a, b, c = 3.0, 4.0, 5.0
K = 6.0
fermat_min = math.sqrt((a*a + b*b + c*c)/2 + 2*math.sqrt(3)*K)
print(f"3-4-5 Fermat min sum: {fermat_min:.4f}")
```

```output
Geologist plane: p=-0.1, q=-0.1, tan(delta)=0.141421, dip=8.05 deg
3-4-5 Fermat min sum: 6.7664
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Check for a $\ge 120^\circ$ Obtuse Angle Before Using the Fermat Formula!**
> If any angle of $\triangle ABC$ is $120^\circ$ or larger (for example, side lengths `1, 1, sqrt(3)`!), the rotated segment $A'C$ passes *outside* the triangle, and $\sqrt{(a^2+b^2+c^2)/2 + 2\sqrt{3}K}$ gives the **wrong** answer! The true minimum sits right on that obtuse vertex ($1 + 1 = 2$).

> [!TRAP]
> **Always Check Which Random Model Is Used for Triangles!**
> Saying "three random points" changes answers wildly with how you sample: uniform around the circle perimeter gives $1/4$ acute; uniform inside a disk gives $1 - \frac{35}{12\pi^2} \approx 0.7045$ obtuse!
