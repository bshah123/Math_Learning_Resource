Stack oranges tightly in a supermarket pyramid, and gaps always remain between the round spheres. Stack identical cubes instead, and every square millimeter fills solidly. **Packing efficiency** measures exactly what fraction of space is filled by non-overlapping congruent spheres or polyhedra:

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Volume of objects inside one repeating cell}}{\text{Total volume of the repeating unit cell}}.$$

In this final geometry lesson, we derive the famous 2D circle fractions ($78.5\%$ vs $90.7\%$) and 3D cubic packing fractions ($52.4\%$, $68.0\%$, $74.0\%$) from simple unit-cell counting, measure the exact tiny spheres (**interstitial voids**) hiding inside the gaps, and see how regular tetrahedra and octahedra tile 3D space edge-to-edge!

## 1. Planar Circle Packing & Cubic Unit Cells

First look on a flat table (2D):
- Arranging equal radius-$r$ circles on a square grid places one full circle ($\pi r^2$) inside a square of side $2r$ ($4r^2$), filling $\pi / 4 \approx 78.54\%$.
- Staggering rows like a honeycomb (**hexagonal packing**) places half an equilateral triangle's tightness: each $2r$-by-$\sqrt{3}r$ rectangle holds $1$ circle, filling $\frac{\pi r^2}{2\sqrt{3}\,r^2} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \approx 90.69\%$—which Thue and Fejes Tóth proved is the **densest possible** equal-circle arrangement in 2D!

Now lift to 3D cubes of edge $a$, remembering how shared atoms along boundaries split between neighboring cells:
- A corner atom is shared by **$8$ cubes** $\implies$ counts **$1/8$**.
- An edge-midpoint atom is shared by **$4$ cubes** $\implies$ counts **$1/4$**.
- A face-center atom is shared by **$2$ cubes** $\implies$ counts **$1/2$**.
- A body-center atom sits strictly inside $\implies$ counts **$1$**.

![Cubic Lattice Unit Cells SC, BCC, FCC](diagrams/16.1-cubic-packings.svg "Shared corner (1/8), face (1/2), and center (1) counts determine SC (1), BCC (2), and FCC (4) sphere counts")

Look carefully along the direction where spheres actually *touch* in each lattice—that single touching line links cell edge $a$ to sphere radius $r$:

| Lattice | Spheres per Cell | Where Spheres Touch! | Edge $a$ | Packing Efficiency | Nearest Neighbors |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple Cubic (SC)** | $8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1$ | Along cube edge | $2r$ | $\frac{\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}{(2r)^3} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 52.4\%$ | $6$ |
| **Body-Centered (BCC)** | $8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 1 = 2$ | Along **body** diagonal | $\sqrt{3}a = 4r \implies a = \frac{4r}{\sqrt{3}}$ | $\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{8} \approx 68.0\%$ | $8$ |
| **Face-Centered (FCC)** | $8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 4$ | Along **face** diagonal | $\sqrt{2}a = 4r \implies a = 2\sqrt{2}\,r$ | $\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} \approx 74.0\%$ | $12$ |

- **FCC vs HCP & Kepler's Conjecture**: Stacking hexagonal supermarket layers in an `ABCABC...` offset produces FCC; stacking them `ABAB...` produces **Hexagonal Close Packing (HCP)**—both filling the exact same $\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} \approx 74.05\%$ of space! Johannes Kepler guessed in 1611 that no arrangement of equal spheres in 3D can beat $\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}}$, a claim finally proved by Thomas Hales in 1998!

## 2. Hiding Small Spheres Inside the Gaps: Interstitial Voids

Look inside an FCC unit cell of $4$ big spheres of radius $R$ (`a = 2*sqrt(2)*R`). Empty pockets sit between them in two shapes—and in a close packing of $N$ big spheres, there are always **$N$ octahedral voids** and **$2N$ tetrahedral voids**!
- **Where are the voids in FCC?** The **$4$ octahedral voids** sit at the cube's body center ($1$) and $12$ edge midpoints ($12 \times 1/4 = 3$, totaling $1 + 3 = 4$!). The **$8$ tetrahedral voids** sit inside each mini-$a/2$ octant corner at $(\pm 1/4, \pm 1/4, \pm 1/4)$.

How large can a small guest sphere of radius $r$ be before it pushes the big host spheres apart?
1. **Octahedral void** ($6$ hosts forming a regular octahedron of edge $2R$): From Lesson 14.1, an octahedron of edge $2R$ has circumradius (distance from pocket center to each host center!) equal to $(2R)\sqrt{2}/2 = R\sqrt{2}$. Since a host reaches inward by $R$, the empty gap radius is $r = R\sqrt{2} - R$, giving critical ratio:
   $$\frac{r}{R} = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142.$$
2. **Tetrahedral void** ($4$ hosts forming a regular tetrahedron of edge $2R$): Circumradius is $(2R)\sqrt{6}/4 = R\sqrt{3/2}$, leaving empty gap:
   $$\frac{r}{R} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} - 1 \approx 0.2247.$$
3. **Cubic void** (sitting right in the center of a Simple Cubic $2R$-cube): Half body diagonal is $R\sqrt{3}$, leaving empty gap:
   $$\frac{r}{R} = \sqrt{3} - 1 \approx 0.7321.$$

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Rock Salt ($\text{NaCl}$) at Critical Radius Ratio Beats $74\%$!**
> Imagine big spheres of radius $r_-$ on the $4$ FCC sites, and small spheres of exact critical size $r_+ = (\sqrt{2} - 1)r_-$ slipped neatly into all $4$ octahedral voids! Because the little spheres only touch along the cube edge ($a = 2r_- + 2r_+ = 2\sqrt{2}\,r_-$, exact match with face diagonal!), nothing overlaps! Adding all $4$ big and $4$ small spheres inside volume $a^3 = (2\sqrt{2}\,r_-)^3$ lifts total packing efficiency from $74.05\%$ up to:
> $$\frac{4 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi \big(r_-^3 + (\sqrt{2}-1)^3 r_-^3\big)}{16\sqrt{2}\,r_-^3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}}\big(1 + 5\sqrt{2} - 7\big) = \frac{\pi}{3}(5 - 3\sqrt{2}) \approx \mathbf{79.31\%}!$$

## 3. Packing Octahedra & Space-Filling Honeycombs

Can regular shapes other than cubes pack 3D space solidly with zero empty air?
- **Regular octahedra alone cannot**: Remember from Lesson 14.1 that a regular octahedron has dihedral angle $\arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47^\circ$. Try wrapping octahedra around a shared edge: $3 \times 109.47^\circ = 328.4^\circ < 360^\circ$, and $4$ won't fit! That leaves a narrow $31.5^\circ$ sliver—Wait: Hermann Minkowski proved (1904, statement only) that the optimal lattice packing of regular octahedra hits density **$18/19 \approx 94.74\%$**! *(Remember from Lesson 7.1: a regular octahedron is literally the 3D Manhattan $\ell_1$ unit ball $|x| + |y| + |z| \le 1$, linking crystal geometry back to metric balls!)*
- **Tetrahedra + Octahedra together tile 3D space 100%**: Since a regular tetrahedron's angle $70.53^\circ$ and an octahedron's angle $109.47^\circ$ add to **$180^\circ$**, alternating **two tetrahedra and two octahedra** around every edge sums to $2(70.53^\circ) + 2(109.47^\circ) = \mathbf{360^\circ}$! With $2$ tetrahedra per octahedron and $4\times$ volume per octahedron, octahedra fill $2/3$ of space and tetrahedra fill $1/3$—the exact dual skeleton of FCC close packing!
- **Space-filling Voronoi cells**: Two semi-regular polyhedra tile 3D space all by themselves cleanly: the **truncated octahedron** (Voronoi cell of BCC) and the **rhombic dodecahedron** (Voronoi cell of FCC).

## 4. Python Unit-Cell & Packing Verifier

Let us verify all four 3D packing efficiencies and three void ratios in Python:

```python
import math

sc = math.pi / 6
bcc = math.pi * math.sqrt(3) / 8
fcc = math.pi / (3 * math.sqrt(2))
rock_salt = (math.pi / 3) * (5 - 3 * math.sqrt(2))

print(f"SC={sc:.4%}, BCC={bcc:.4%}, FCC={fcc:.4%}, RockSalt={rock_salt:.4%}")
print(f"Void ratios r/R -> oct: {math.sqrt(2)-1:.4f}, tet: {math.sqrt(1.5)-1:.4f}, cubic: {math.sqrt(3)-1:.4f}")
```

```output
SC=52.3599%, BCC=68.0175%, FCC=74.0480%, RockSalt=79.3105%
Void ratios r/R -> oct: 0.4142, tet: 0.2247, cubic: 0.7321
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Check Which Line Spheres Touch Along!**
> Never plug $a = 2r$ blindly into every cubic crystal! Spheres touch along an **edge** in Simple Cubic ($a = 2r$), along the **body** diagonal in BCC ($\sqrt{3}a = 4r$), and along a **face** diagonal in FCC ($\sqrt{2}a = 4r$). Drawing one diagonal slice prevents every factor-of-$\sqrt{2}$ error!

## Exercises

2. An ionic crystal puts anions of radius $R = 1.00\,\text{\AA}$ on an FCC grid. Which void pockets (tetrahedral or octahedral) can fit a cation of radius $r = 0.35\,\text{\AA}$ without pushing the anions apart?

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. The center atom touches every corner atom directly along the half body diagonal, so center-to-corner distance is simply $r + r = 2r = 2$. Meanwhile the full body diagonal equals $4r = 4$, giving $\sqrt{3}\,a = 4 \implies a = \frac{4}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 2.3094$.
2. Ratio $r / R = 0.35$. A tetrahedral void only accommodates up to $\sqrt{3/2} - 1 \approx 0.2247$ (too small!), whereas an octahedral void accommodates up to $\sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142$! So the $0.35\,\text{\AA}$ cation fits cleanly inside an **octahedral void**!
