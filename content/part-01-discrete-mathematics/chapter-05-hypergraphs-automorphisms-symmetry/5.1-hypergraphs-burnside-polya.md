In an ordinary graph, every edge links just two vertices. What if we allow a "line" or relationship to connect **three, four, or any subset** of vertices at once—such as the three winning squares along a row of tic-tac-toe, or three points collinear on a plane? That broader structure is a **hypergraph**.

Just as in Lesson 4.1, two natural questions arise right away: first, how many symmetries (**automorphisms**) leave a hypergraph unchanged? Using **Orbit-Stabilizer** from Toolkit Lesson 0.1 gives an exact count without brute-force guessing. Second, how many distinct ways can we color beads on a necklace or faces on a cube when rotated copies count as the same? That leads us straight to **Burnside's Lemma** and **Pólya's cycle-index trick**.

## 1. Hypergraphs and Finite Geometries

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Hypergraph, $k$-Uniformity & Incidence Matrix**
> A **hypergraph** $H = (V, E)$ consists of a vertex set $V$ and a family $E$ of subsets of $V$ called **hyperedges** (or *lines*). If every hyperedge contains exactly $k$ vertices, $H$ is **$k$-uniform** (so ordinary graphs are $2$-uniform!). The **degree** $\deg(v)$ counts how many hyperedges contain $v$, and the **incidence matrix** writes a $1$ in entry $(v, e)$ when $v \in e$ and $0$ otherwise.

Three benchmark hypergraphs appear constantly in finite geometry and competition combinatorics:
- **Tic-Tac-Toe Board ($n \times n$)**: $n^2$ cells as vertices, and $2n + 2$ winning lines ($n$ rows, $n$ columns, $2$ main diagonals) as $n$-point hyperedges.
- **The Fano Plane**: The smallest projective plane—just $7$ points and $7$ lines of $3$ points each! Every pair of points determines a **unique** line, and every pair of lines meets at a **unique** point. (Because the 7th 3-point line cannot be drawn straight in flat Euclidean geometry, it is traditionally drawn as the circle passing through the $3$ edge midpoints!)
- **Affine Plane of Order $3$**: Take a $3 \times 3$ grid of $9$ points and $12$ lines of size $3$: $3$ rows, $3$ columns, $3$ wrap-around slopes $+1$ diagonals, and $3$ wrap-around slope $-1$ diagonals. Every pair of points lies on a unique line, and $9$ points divide cleanly into $4$ parallel directions of $3$ lines each.
- **Steiner Triple Systems (Kirkman's Theorem, statement only)**: A $3$-uniform hypergraph where every pair of vertices lies in a unique triple exists on $n$ vertices if and only if $n \equiv 1 \text{ or } 3 \pmod 6$ (note $n = 7$ is the Fano plane, $n = 9$ is an affine plane of order $3$ minus one line direction—wait: in affine order 3, each line has 3 points and every pair of points determines 1 unique line, so all $12$ lines already form $STS(9)$!).

## 2. Finding Automorphisms Without Missing Hidden Ones

An **automorphism** of a hypergraph is a bijection $\sigma : V \to V$ sending hyperedges to hyperedges. Because $\sigma$ is a symmetry of the entire structure, it must preserve **every** invariant: degrees, edge sizes, and which points lie together on a line!

Let us work through three eye-opening examples of Orbit-Stabilizer in action:

1. **$3 \times 3$ Tic-Tac-Toe Board**: Look at the vertex degrees: the center sits on $4$ winning lines, the $4$ corners sit on $3$ lines each, and the $4$ edge midpoints sit on only $2$ lines each. Therefore *every* automorphism must keep the center fixed, permute the corners among corners, and map two corners on a border line to two corners on a border line (around the outer $4$-cycle). Pick where one corner goes ($4$ choices) and where its adjacent clockwise corner goes ($2$ choices along the square perimeter), forcing the opposite corners and every midpoint cleanly! So $|\operatorname{Aut}| = 4 \times 2 = 8$, the exact $8$ geometric symmetries of a square.
2. **Wait—$4 \times 4$ Tic-Tac-Toe Has Hidden Symmetries!**: On a $4 \times 4$ board, swapping **rows 2 and 3** AND simultaneously swapping **columns 2 and 3** keeps every row, every column, and both diagonals intact as sets—even though corners stayed fixed and the board was *torn and reassembled* inside! Doing that non-geometric fold on rows/cols $\{1,4\}$ vs $\{2,3\}$ multiplies the $8$ geometric symmetries by $4$, giving $|\operatorname{Aut}| = 32$ symmetries! Never assume geometric rigid motions are the whole group!
3. **The Fano Plane ($|\operatorname{Aut}| = 168$)**: Choose where point $A$ goes: all $7$ points look identical, so there are **$7$ choices** for $\sigma(A)$. Now choose where a second point $B$ goes: any of the remaining **$6$ choices** works. Once $\sigma(A)$ and $\sigma(B)$ are chosen, the line through $AB$ (including its 3rd point!) is completely locked in! Next pick a third point $C$ *not* on line $AB$: it must go to any of the $7 - 3 =$ **$4$ points** outside line $\sigma(A)\sigma(B)$. Once $A, B, C$ are locked, every remaining point is uniquely trapped as the intersection of two forced lines (`AC` and `BC` give two more points, and lines through them give the last!). Multiplying choices via Orbit-Stabilizer gives:
   $$|\operatorname{Aut}(\text{Fano})| = 7 \times 6 \times 4 = 168.$$

## 3. Burnside's Lemma & Pólya Counting (Coloring Without Overcounting)

Suppose you want to count how many distinct $2$-color necklaces you can string with $6$ beads, treating two necklaces as identical if rotating one turns it into the other. There are $2^6 = 64$ labeled colorings, yet dividing by $6$ gives $64/6 = 10.666\dots$—not even an integer! Why? Because uniform all-red `RRRRRR` only makes $1$ pattern under rotation, whereas `RBRBRB` makes $2$, and `RRRRRB` makes $6$.

Burnside's Lemma turns that headache into an exact average over group elements:

> [!THEOREM]
> **Burnside's Lemma**
> Let a finite symmetry group $G$ act on a finite set of objects $X$, and let $\operatorname{Fix}(g) = \{x \in X : gx = x\}$ be the set of objects left looking *identical* after applying move $g$. Then the number of distinct symmetry classes (**orbits**) is simply the *average* number of fixed objects:
> $$\#\{\text{orbits}\} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} |\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.$$

Let us see how **Orbit-Stabilizer** proves Burnside's Lemma in three clean steps:

> [!PROOF]
> Make a giant table counting all pairs $(g, x) \in G \times X$ such that $gx = x$, in two ways:
> - **Row by row (by group element $g$)**: Each $g$ contributes $|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$, so total pairs $= \sum_{g \in G} |\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$.
> - **Column by column (by object $x$)**: Each $x$ contributes $|G_x|$, the size of its stabilizer. By Orbit-Stabilizer (Lesson 0.1), $|G_x| = |G| / |Gx|$. Now group all items $x$ belonging to the same orbit $O$ (so $|Gx| = |O|$ for every $x \in O$):
> $$\sum_{x \in O} |G_x| = \sum_{x \in O} \frac{|G|}{|O|} = |O| \cdot \frac{|G|}{|O|} = |G|.$$
> So *each orbit contributes exactly $|G|$*! Adding across all orbits gives $\#\{\text{orbits}\} \cdot |G| = \sum_{g \in G} |\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$, and dividing by $|G|$ completes the proof.

**Pólya's Coloring Trick**: When $G$ permutes positions (like $6$ bead spots) and we color each position using $k$ colors, when does a coloring stay unchanged by permutation $g$? Whenever every bead inside the **same cycle** of $g$ gets painted the exact same color! If $g$ breaks the positions into $c(g)$ disjoint cycles, there are $k^{c(g)}$ fixed colorings: $|\operatorname{Fix}(g)| = k^{c(g)}$.

Even better, to track exact color budgets (say $r$ red beads and $b$ blue beads), write each cycle of length $i$ as $(r^i + b^i)$ inside the **cycle index** $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G}\prod_i x_i^{c_i(g)}$, expand, and read off the coefficient of $r^a b^c$!

## 4. Concrete Necklaces, Bracelets & Cube Faces

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **6-Bead Necklaces vs Bracelets in 2 Colors**
> - **Necklaces ($n = 6$, rotations only, $|C_6| = 6$)**: Rotating by $r$ spots splits $n$ beads into $\gcd(n, r)$ cycles, giving the universal formula $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d \mid n}\varphi(d)k^{n/d}$. For $n=6, k=2$: shifts $r=0,1,2,3,4,5$ have cycle counts $6, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1$, fixing $2^6 + 2^1 + 2^2 + 2^3 + 2^2 + 2^1 = 64 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 84$ colorings $\implies 84 / 6 = 14$ distinct necklaces.
> - **Bracelets ($n = 6$, rotations + flips, $|D_6| = 12$)**: You can now also flip the necklace over! Three flips pass through two opposite beads (fixing those $2$ beads in $1$-cycles and pairing the remaining $4$ into $2$-cycles $\implies 4$ cycles total, $2^4 = 16$ fixed colorings each), and three flips cut between beads (pairing all $6$ beads $\implies 3$ cycles, $2^3 = 8$ each). Total fixed $= 84 + 3(16) + 3(8) = 156 \implies 156 / 12 = 13$ bracelets (because one chiral pair of necklaces turns into each other when flipped!).

- **Exact counts via Cycle Index**: For $4$-bead necklaces ($C_4$), shifts have types $x_1^4$ (identity), $x_2^2$ ($180^\circ$), and two $x_4$ ($90^\circ, 270^\circ$). To count necklaces with $2$ red and $2$ blue beads, substitute $x_i = r^i + b^i$ into $\frac{1}{4}(x_1^4 + x_2^2 + 2x_4)$ and pick the coefficient of $r^2 b^2$: $\frac{1}{4}\big(\binom{4}{2} + \binom{2}{1} + 0\big) = \frac{6 + 2}{4} = 2$ (`RRBB` and `RBRB`).
- **Painting Cube Faces with $k$ Colors (24 Rotations)**: Categorize the $24$ rotations by how they cycle the $6$ faces: $1$ identity ($6$ cycles), $6$ face-$90^\circ$ turns ($3$ cycles), $3$ face-$180^\circ$ turns ($4$ cycles), $8$ corner-diagonal $120^\circ$ turns ($2$ cycles), and $6$ edge-$180^\circ$ turns ($3$ cycles). Averaging gives $\frac{k^6 + 3k^4 + 12k^3 + 8k^2}{24}$, which for $k = 3$ colors equals $57$ distinct painted cubes!

## 5. Python Burnside Verifier

Let us verify the necklace, bracelet, and cube-face counts right now in Python:

```python
from math import gcd

def necklaces(n, k):
    return sum(k ** gcd(n, r) for r in range(n)) // n

def bracelets(n, k):
    rot = sum(k ** gcd(n, r) for r in range(n))
    if n % 2 == 1:
        refl = n * (k ** ((n + 1) // 2))
    else:
        refl = (n // 2) * (k ** (n // 2 + 1)) + (n // 2) * (k ** (n // 2))
    return (rot + refl) // (2 * n)

def cube_faces(k):
    return (k**6 + 3*k**4 + 12*k**3 + 8*k**2) // 24

print("6 beads, 2 colors -> necklaces:", necklaces(6, 2), "bracelets:", bracelets(6, 2))
print("Cube face colorings for k=2,3,4:", [cube_faces(k) for k in (2, 3, 4)])
```

```output
6 beads, 2 colors -> necklaces: 14 bracelets: 13
Cube face colorings for k=2,3,4: [10, 57, 240]
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Matching Degrees Alone Does Not Make an Automorphism**
> A vertex bijection is an automorphism *only* if every hyperedge lands cleanly on another hyperedge! At the same time, never assume rigid geometry captures everything—hidden combinatorial flips like swapping the two inner rows & columns of a $4 \times 4$ board can quadruple the symmetry group!

> [!TRAP]
> **Always Clarify Necklaces vs Bracelets**
> Rotations only ($C_n$, necklaces) vs allowing physical flipping ($D_n$, bracelets) produces different counts whenever patterns aren't mirror-symmetric. Always check whether reflections belong in $G$ before running Burnside's average.
