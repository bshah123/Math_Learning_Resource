Suppose two crowded movie theaters let out at the same time, and you want to know whether both theaters held the exact same number of people—without counting either crowd one by one. How could you tell? Ask everyone from Theater A to hold hands with one partner from Theater B! If every single person gets an exclusive partner and nobody is left standing alone, the two crowds *must* have the exact same size.

That everyday idea—pairing elements one-to-one—is called a **bijection**, and together with **double counting** (adding up the same table of numbers by rows and then by columns), it replaces blind algebra with effortless visual insight.

## 1. Bijections, Double Counting & Symmetry Division

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Bijection (One-to-One Correspondence)**
> A function $f : A \to B$ is a **bijection** if it is both **injective** (two different items in $A$ never land on the same target in $B$) and **surjective** (every item in $B$ is hit by at least one item in $A$).
> In practice, the cleanest way to prove $f$ is a bijection is to construct its **inverse rule** $f^{-1} : B \to A$ and verify both round trips: $f^{-1}(f(a)) = a$ and $f(f^{-1}(b)) = b$.

Notice two companion counting habits that work alongside bijections:
- **Double counting**: Put pairs $(x, y)$ into a two-way table (for example, $x$ is a club member and $y$ is a club they belong to, with a checkmark when $x \in y$). Counting checkmarks row by row equals counting checkmarks column by column—producing surprising identities automatically!
- **Symmetry division rule**: If a symmetry group $G$ turns arrangements into equivalent ones (like rotating seats around a circular table), dividing the ordered count by $|G|$ is valid **only when every arrangement changes into $|G|$ distinct versions**. As soon as some symmetric pattern maps back onto itself (like a checkerboard necklace under rotation), simple division breaks—and you need Burnside's Lemma (Lesson 5.1).

## 2. Seven Classic Families Every Competitor Should Recognize

Look how one counting picture reappears across completely different stories:

| Target Objects | Matched Objects (Bijection) | Exact Count |
|---|---|---|
| Subsets of $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ | Binary strings of length $n$ (`1` = inside, `0` = outside) | $2^n$ |
| Nonnegative solutions of $x_1 + \dots + x_k = n$ | Rows of $n$ identical stars and $k - 1$ dividing bars | $\binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}$ |
| Positive solutions ($x_i \ge 1$) of $x_1 + \dots + x_k = n$ | Placing $k - 1$ bars into the $n - 1$ gaps between $n$ stars | $\binom{n - 1}{k - 1}$ |
| East/North lattice paths $(0,0) \to (n,n)$ never crossing above $y = x$ | Valid bracket strings of $n$ `()` pairs; full binary trees with $n+1$ leaves; polygon triangulations of a convex $(n+2)$-gon | $C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$ |
| Labeled trees on $\{1, \dots, n\}$ | Sequences of length $n - 2$ from $\{1, \dots, n\}$ (Prüfer codes, Lesson 3.2) | $n^{n-2}$ |
| Partitions of $n$ into $\le k$ parts | Partitions of $n$ with largest part $\le k$ (flip Ferrers diagram diagonally) | Equal |
| Partitions of $n$ into distinct parts | Partitions of $n$ into odd parts (Glaisher's binary doubling, Lesson 3.2) | Equal |

## 3. Catalan Numbers and the Mirror Reflection Trick

Think of walking across a city grid from $(0, 0)$ to $(n, n)$ by taking $n$ steps **East ($E$)** and $n$ steps **North ($N$)**—that is $2n$ steps in all, with $\binom{2n}{n}$ possible paths. Now forbid yourself from ever stepping **above** the main diagonal $y = x$ (meaning your total $N$ steps can never outnumber your $E$ steps so far, just like a closing parenthesis `)` can never appear before a matching `(`!).

Let us check small values by hand first:
- For $n = 1$: Only `EN` stays on or below $y = x$ (`NE` crosses!). So $C_1 = 1$.
- For $n = 2$: Out of $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ total paths, only `EENN` and `ENEN` behave, giving $C_2 = 2$.
- For $n = 3$: Out of $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ paths, listing safe paths gives $C_3 = 5$. Notice the sequence $1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132$: each equals $\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$! Why?

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Reflecting the Bad Suffix Makes Counting Effortless**
> Instead of trying to track valid paths, let us count the **bad** paths—the ones that step onto the forbidden danger line $y = x + 1$! Look at the very **first moment** a bad path touches $y = x + 1$. Take everything the path does *after* that first touch, and reflect it across the mirror line $y = x + 1$ (turning every future East step into a North step and every future North step into an East step). Because reflection preserves the first touchpoint and swaps remaining Easts with remaining Norths, a reflected path finishing at $(n, n)$ before must end at $(n - 1, n + 1)$ after! Conversely, *every* path from $(0, 0)$ to $(n - 1, n + 1)$ starts below $y = x + 1$ and ends above it, so it *must* cross $y = x + 1$ somewhere for the first time—and flipping its suffix brings it uniquely back to a bad path at $(n, n)$!

![Catalan Reflection Principle Across y = x + 1](diagrams/3.1-catalan-reflection.svg "Reflecting the suffix after the first touch of y = x + 1 maps bad paths bijectively to (n-1, n+1)")

> [!THEOREM]
> **Catalan Formula & Bertrand's Ballot Theorem**
> 1. **Catalan Numbers**: The number of East/North paths from $(0, 0)$ to $(n, n)$ staying on or below $y = x$ is:
> $$C_n = \binom{2n}{n} - \binom{2n}{n - 1} = \frac{1}{n + 1}\binom{2n}{n}.$$
> 2. **Ballot Theorem**: Suppose in an election, candidate A finishes with $a$ votes and candidate B finishes with $b < a$ votes, and ballots are shuffled into a uniformly random counting order. The probability that A stays **strictly ahead** of B after *every single ballot* is:
> $$P(\text{A strictly ahead throughout}) = \frac{a - b}{a + b}.$$

Let us verify the Ballot Theorem with the exact same mirror trick! For A to lead strictly after ballot $1$, the very first vote must go to A, moving our count from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$ (or equivalently keeping $a - 1$ A-votes and $b$ B-votes ahead). From $(1, 0)$ to $(a, b)$, a sequence fails only if A's total ever drops down to tie B—touching the line $y = x$! Reflecting the portion *after* the first touch of $y = x$ swaps $E$ and $N$ steps from $(1, 0)$ into $(0, 1)$, creating a bijection onto *all* paths from $(0, 1)$ to $(a, b)$! Subtracting gives winning paths:
$$\binom{a + b - 1}{b} - \binom{a + b - 1}{b - 1} = \binom{a + b}{a} \cdot \frac{a - b}{a + b}.$$
Test it on $a = 3, b = 2$: total ways to order `AAABB` is $\binom{5}{2} = 10$. The formula predicts $\frac{3 - 2}{3 + 2} = \frac{1}{5}$, i.e., $2$ winning orders—and indeed only `AAABB` and `AABAB` keep A strictly ahead at every vote!

## 4. Two Classic Double-Counting Identities

When faced with a binomial sum, try inventing a tiny committee-selection story counted in two ways:

1. **Committees with a Chairperson**: $\sum_{k=0}^n k \binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$. *Story*: From $n$ club members, form a committee of any size and appoint one committee member as chair. **Way 1**: Pick the $k$-person committee first in $\binom{n}{k}$ ways, then pick its chair in $k$ ways, and sum over $k$. **Way 2**: Pick the chair first out of all $n$ people ($n$ ways), then let any subset of the remaining $n - 1$ people join the committee ($2^{n-1}$ ways).
2. **Vandermonde's Identity**: $\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$. *Story*: Pick a team of $n$ students from $n$ juniors and $n$ seniors. **Way 1**: Select $n$ directly from $2n$ in $\binom{2n}{n}$ ways. **Way 2**: Pick $k$ juniors and $n - k$ seniors in $\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{n-k} = \binom{n}{k}^2$ ways, summing $k = 0 \dots n$.

## 5. Verification in Python

Let us print $C_1 \dots C_6$ and confirm the Ballot Theorem probability $\frac{a-b}{a+b}$ for $a = 3, b = 2$ by inspecting all $10$ permutations explicitly.

```python
import math
from itertools import permutations

# 1. Verify Catalan numbers C_1..C_6
print("Catalan C_1..C_6:", [math.comb(2 * n, n) // (n + 1) for n in range(1, 7)])

# 2. Verify Ballot Theorem for a=3, b=2 by brute force over all distinct voting sequences
orders = set(permutations(["A", "A", "A", "B", "B"]))
strictly_ahead = 0
for seq in orders:
    diff, ok = 0, True
    for vote in seq:
        diff += 1 if vote == "A" else -1
        if diff <= 0:
            ok = False
            break
    if ok:
        strictly_ahead += 1
print(f"Ballot (a=3, b=2): {strictly_ahead}/{len(orders)} = {strictly_ahead/len(orders)} vs (a-b)/(a+b)={(3-2)/(3+2)}")
```

```output
Catalan C_1..C_6: [1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132]
Ballot (a=3, b=2): 2/10 = 0.2 vs (a-b)/(a+b)=0.2
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Nonnegative vs Positive Integer Solutions in Stars and Bars**
> Think carefully before applying $\binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}$: that counts solutions where variables *can be zero* ($x_i \ge 0$). If every box must get **at least one** item ($x_i \ge 1$), hand out $1$ item to each of the $k$ boxes upfront, leaving $n - k$ items to split freely ($y_i = x_i - 1 \ge 0$) in $\binom{(n-k) + k - 1}{k - 1} = \binom{n - 1}{k - 1}$ ways.

> [!TRAP]
> **Checking Both Halves of a Bijection**
> Never wave your hands and declare "clearly a bijection!" Always verify two concrete details: (1) every legal input maps *inside* the target set (no out-of-bounds images), and (2) the inverse rule takes every target back inside the domain uniquely.
