In Lesson 1.1, Euler's problem asked us to travel across every **edge** of a map once—and checking even degrees gave a complete yes-or-no answer in seconds. Now ask what seems like a twin question: can you plan a delivery route that visits every **vertex** (city) exactly once?

Such a tour is called a **Hamiltonian path** (if you stop at the last city) or a **Hamiltonian cycle** (if you return home). Despite sounding nearly identical to Euler trails, Hamiltonian cycles are famously difficult: nobody knows a fast universal test for them. Instead, problem solvers carry two complementary toolkits—**three quick obstructions** to prove a graph has *no* cycle, and **three structural theorems** that guarantee one exists.

## 1. Counting Cycles on Complete and Bipartite Graphs

Before hunting for cycles in tricky graphs, let us make sure we share the same counting rule. For an undirected graph, traversing a cycle `1-2-3-4-1` is the exact same cycle as traversing `2-3-4-1-2` (shifting the start) or `1-4-3-2-1` (going backwards).

- On $K_n$ ($n \ge 3$): There are $(n - 1)!$ circular arrangements around a table, and reversing direction gives the same undirected cycle, leaving $\frac{(n-1)!}{2}$ Hamiltonian cycles.
- On $K_{n,n}$ ($n \ge 2$): Alternate between Left and Right vertices! Pick a fixed starting vertex $L_1$, then alternate picking the remaining $n-1$ Left vertices and $n$ Right vertices—which gives $(n-1)!\, n!$ directed tours, or $\frac{n!(n-1)!}{2}$ undirected Hamiltonian cycles.

## 2. Three Quick Obstructions (Proving No Cycle Exists)

Whenever you suspect a graph has *no* Hamiltonian cycle, do not waste time searching paths—check these three structural bottlenecks first:

1. **The Components (Vertex-Deletion) Test**: Imagine a working Hamiltonian cycle—essentially one big circle of $n$ vertices. If you erase any $k$ vertices from that circle, how many broken pieces (connected arcs) can remain? At most $k$! Therefore, if removing a nonempty set $S \subseteq V$ breaks $G$ into **more than $|S|$ connected components**, then $G$ cannot possibly have a Hamiltonian cycle. *(For a Hamiltonian path—an open line segment rather than a loop—cutting $k$ vertices leaves at most $k + 1$ pieces.)*
2. **Bipartite Balance Test**: As you walk around a Hamiltonian cycle inside a bipartite graph, your color must flip at every single step: `Red - Blue - Red - Blue ...`. To close back onto the start, the graph **must have equally many Red and Blue vertices**. For a Hamiltonian path, sizes can differ by at most $1$—and if they differ by $1$, the path is forced to start and end in the larger color!
3. **Forced Degree-2 Edges**: If a vertex has degree $2$, a Hamiltonian cycle visiting it must enter via one edge and exit via the other—meaning **both** of its edges are forced into every Hamiltonian cycle. If those forced edges accidentally close a shorter cycle of length $< n$, or force $3$ edges onto a single vertex, no Hamiltonian cycle can exist!

## 3. When Are Edges Dense Enough? Ore, Dirac & Rédei

Now flip the question around: if every city connects to enough neighbors, must a Hamiltonian cycle appear automatically? Notice first that minimum degree alone needs at least $n/2$: take two separate clusters of size $k$ sharing a single bridge, or take $K_{k, k+1}$ on $n = 2k + 1$ vertices (minimum degree $k = (n-1)/2$), which fails bipartite balance! Ore's 1960 theorem proves that $\deg u + \deg v \ge n$ on non-neighbors is already enough:

> [!THEOREM]
> **Ore's Theorem & Dirac's Theorem**
> - **Ore's Theorem**: If $G$ has $n \ge 3$ vertices and $\deg u + \deg v \ge n$ for every pair of **non-adjacent** vertices $u$ and $v$, then $G$ has a Hamiltonian cycle.
> - **Dirac's Theorem (Corollary)**: If $n \ge 3$ and every vertex has degree at least $n/2$, then $G$ has a Hamiltonian cycle. *(Clearly every non-adjacent pair has sum $\ge n/2 + n/2 = n$!)*

Look carefully at the clever **"maximal counterexample + flip trick"** inside Ore's proof—it is one of the cleanest arguments in combinatorics:

> [!PROOF]
> Suppose for contradiction that Ore's condition holds on some $n$-vertex graph $G$ ($n \ge 3$), yet $G$ has no Hamiltonian cycle. Keep adding missing edges to $G$ one by one as long as no Hamiltonian cycle appears—stopping right before an added edge would create one. Adding edges only increases degrees, so the condition $\deg u + \deg v \ge n$ still holds on this maximal non-Hamiltonian graph $G'$.
>
> Since $G' \neq K_n$, pick two non-adjacent vertices $u$ and $v$. By our stopping rule, adding the single edge $uv$ creates a Hamiltonian cycle, which means $G'$ already contains a Hamiltonian path starting at $u$ and ending at $v$:
> $$u = v_1 - v_2 - v_3 - \dots - v_{n-1} - v_n = v.$$
> Now look for a twist that closes this path into a cycle! If there exists an index $i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$ such that $v_1$ connects to $v_{i+1}$ AND $v_i$ connects to $v_n$, then we can rewire the path into a full cycle:
> $$v_1 \to v_{i+1} \to v_{i+2} \to \dots \to v_n \to v_i \to v_{i-1} \to \dots \to v_2 \to v_1.$$
> Since $G'$ has no Hamiltonian cycle, no such index $i$ can exist! In other words, the set $A = \{i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\} : v_1 \sim v_{i+1}\}$ (which has size $\deg u$) and the set $B = \{i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\} : v_i \sim v_n\}$ (which has size $\deg v$) must be completely disjoint subsets of the $(n-1)$-element set $\{1, \dots, n-1\}$. Thus $\deg u + \deg v \le n - 1$, contradicting $\deg u + \deg v \ge n$!

What about **tournaments**—complete graphs where every match between two players has a winner arrow $u \to v$? Even without any degree threshold, you can always rank all $n$ players in a line so each beats the next!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Rédei's Theorem (Every Tournament Has a Directed Hamiltonian Path)**
> Every tournament on $n$ vertices contains at least one directed Hamiltonian path $v_1 \to v_2 \to \dots \to v_n$. *(In fact, the number of such paths is always odd, and a tournament has a directed cycle through all vertices iff it is strongly connected—Camion's theorem.)*

> [!PROOF]
> Induct on $n$. For $n = 1$ a single vertex works. Suppose the first $n - 1$ players form a directed chain $v_1 \to v_2 \to \dots \to v_{n-1}$, and bring in player $x$:
> - If $x \to v_1$, place $x$ at the very front: $x \to v_1 \to \dots \to v_{n-1}$.
> - If $v_{n-1} \to x$, place $x$ at the very back: $v_1 \to \dots \to v_{n-1} \to x$.
> - Otherwise we must have $v_1 \to x$ at the start and $x \to v_{n-1}$ at the end. Look at the arrows between the chain and $x$ from left to right: because the chain starts beating $x$ and ends losing to $x$, there is a first vertex $v_{i+1}$ that loses to $x$ ($x \to v_{i+1}$), right after a vertex $v_i$ that beat $x$ ($v_i \to x$)! Simply splice $x$ right between $v_i$ and $v_{i+1}$.

## 4. Reference Table & Binary Reflected Gray Codes

Keep this quick lookup table handy when classic graphs appear on exams:

| Graph Family | Hamiltonian Cycle? | Why / Key Construction |
|---|---|---|
| $K_n$ ($n \ge 3$) | Yes | $(n-1)!/2$ cycles |
| $K_{m,n}$ | Only when $m = n \ge 2$ | Bipartite balance forces $m = n$ |
| Hypercube $Q_n$ ($n \ge 2$) | Yes | Binary reflected Gray codes |
| Grid $P_m \times P_n$ ($m, n \ge 2$) | Exactly when $mn$ is even | Chessboard coloring needs equal Black/White squares |
| Petersen graph | No | Has a Hamiltonian path, famous $10$-vertex counterexample |
| Knight's graph ($m \times n$, $m \le n$) | Yes, except $m,n$ both odd; $m \in \{1,2,4\}$; or $m=3, n \in \{4,6,8\}$ | Chessboard parity + Pósa's $4 \times n$ trick |

How do you build a **Gray code**—a Hamiltonian cycle on the hypercube $Q_n$ visiting all $2^n$ binary strings by changing only $1$ bit per step? Take the $(n-1)$-bit list, stick `0` in front of each string going forward, and stick `1` in front of each string in *reverse* order! Why does the seam match? Because the last string of the `0`-half and the first string of the reversed `1`-half share the exact same $(n-1)$-bit suffix and differ only in that first bit! Closed-form rule: the $k$-th string is simply $k \oplus \lfloor k/2 \rfloor$ in binary.

## 5. Worked Examples & Python Check

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Three Obstruction & Edge-Threshold Classics**
> 1. **$5 \times 5$ Chessboard**: Coloring alternating squares gives $13$ black and $12$ white squares. Since every step between adjacent squares switches color, no closed Hamiltonian cycle can visit all $25$ squares. Hamiltonian paths *do* exist, provided they both start and end on black!
> 2. **Pósa's Proof: Why a Knight Can Never Tour a $4 \times n$ Board Closed**: Label rows $1$ and $4$ as **outer** ($2n$ squares) and rows $2$ and $3$ as **inner** ($2n$ squares). Look at where a knight in an outer row can jump: changing row by $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$ from row $1$ or $4$ *always* lands in row $2$ or $3$! So every outer square only connects to inner squares, meaning any closed knight's tour must alternate `outer -> inner -> outer`. Yet every knight move *also* flips chessboard color (Black $\leftrightarrow$ White)! That forces all outer squares to have one color and all inner squares to have the other—contradicting the fact that rows $1$ and $4$ obviously contain both black and white squares!
> 3. **Edge-Count Guarantee**: Prove any graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices with at least $\binom{n-1}{2} + 2$ edges is Hamiltonian. *Proof*: Pick any two non-adjacent vertices $u$ and $v$. Outside $\{u, v\}$ there are $n - 2$ other vertices, containing at most $\binom{n-2}{2}$ edges among themselves. Every remaining edge of the graph must touch $u$ or $v$, so $\deg u + \deg v \ge \big(\binom{n-1}{2} + 2\big) - \binom{n-2}{2} = (n - 2) + 2 = n$. Now Ore's Theorem finishes immediately! (Sharpness: $K_{n-1}$ plus one leaf attached by $1$ edge has $\binom{n-1}{2} + 1$ edges and no cycle.)

Let us verify the $3$-bit Gray code cycle and confirm every consecutive step flips only $1$ bit in Python:

```python
# Generate 3-bit and 4-bit Gray codes via k ^ (k >> 1) and verify single-bit steps
def gray_code(n):
    return [format(k ^ (k >> 1), f'0{n}b') for k in range(1 << n)]

g3 = gray_code(3)
print("3-bit Gray cycle:", " -> ".join(g3))
diffs = [sum(a != b for a, b in zip(g3[i], g3[(i + 1) % len(g3)])) for i in range(len(g3))]
print("Hamming steps around Q3 cycle:", diffs)
```

```output
3-bit Gray cycle: 000 -> 001 -> 011 -> 010 -> 110 -> 111 -> 101 -> 100
Hamming steps around Q3 cycle: [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Sufficient Is Not Necessary**
> Dirac ($\deg \ge n/2$) and Ore ($\deg u + \deg v \ge n$) are one-way guarantees, never requirements. A $100$-cycle $C_{100}$ obviously has a Hamiltonian cycle even though every vertex has degree just $2$.

> [!TRAP]
> **Equal Bipartite Sizes Do Not Prove a Cycle Exists**
> Having equal numbers of Red and Blue vertices is necessary, never sufficient. Also test the Components Test and forced degree-$2$ edges!
