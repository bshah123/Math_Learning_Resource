What do **coloring a map**, **counting walks on a network**, and **finding symmetries of a molecule** all share in common? At first they look like three completely unrelated subjects. Yet all three are special cases of one remarkably simple idea: mapping vertices of one graph $G$ onto vertices of another graph $H$ while keeping every edge connected—a **graph homomorphism**.

In this lesson we unpack homomorphisms from the ground up, see why odd cycles behave like one-way valves, count homomorphisms out of any tree by bottom-up dynamic programming, and use Jordan's tree center from Lesson 1.1 to count tree automorphisms cleanly.

## 1. What Is a Graph Homomorphism?

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Graph Homomorphism & Isomorphism**
> A **graph homomorphism** $f : G \to H$ is a function mapping vertices of $G$ to vertices of $H$ so that whenever two vertices $u$ and $v$ share an edge in $G$, their images $f(u)$ and $f(v)$ **must** share an edge in $H$. (Non-adjacent vertices in $G$ are free to map anywhere—even onto the exact same vertex!)
> We write $G \to H$ if at least one homomorphism exists, and $\operatorname{hom}(G, H)$ for the total number of distinct maps $V(G) \to V(H)$ that satisfy this rule. A bijective homomorphism whose inverse is also a homomorphism is an **isomorphism**; an isomorphism from $G$ to itself is an **automorphism**.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Colorings Are Literally Homomorphisms Into Complete Graphs**
> Imagine $H = K_3$ with three vertices labeled `Red, Blue, Green`, where every color joins every *other* color (no self-loops!). Now assign every vertex of $G$ to one of `{Red, Blue, Green}`. When does an edge $uv$ in $G$ land on an edge in $K_3$? Precisely when $f(u) \neq f(v)$—the exact definition of a proper $3$-coloring! So "properly $k$-coloring $G$" and "mapping $G \to K_k$" are word-for-word identical!

Because edges cannot stretch across non-edges, six structural facts hold automatically:

1. **Composition**: If $G \to H$ and $H \to K$, composing them gives $G \to K$.
2. **Chromatic numbers**: $G \to K_k$ holds iff $G$ is $k$-colorable, so $\chi(G) = \min\{k : G \to K_k\}$. Since $H \to K_k$ implies $G \to K_k$ whenever $G \to H$, any homomorphism forces $\chi(G) \le \chi(H)$.
3. **Cliques**: In a simple graph $H$ (no self-loops), every pair in a clique $K_r \subseteq G$ must stay distinct in $H$, forcing $\omega(G) \le \omega(H)$.
4. **Bipartite collapse**: Any graph $G$ with at least one edge is bipartite iff $G \to K_2$ (sending the two color classes to the two ends of an edge). Conversely $K_2 \to G$ whenever $G$ has an edge!
5. **Odd cycles only map downhill**: $C_{2k+1} \to C_{2m+1}$ holds **if and only if $k \ge m$** (longer odd cycles wrap onto shorter ones, *never* vice versa!). *Why?* For $k \ge m$, walk once around the $2m+1$ vertices and then bounce back and forth along one edge for the remaining $2(k-m)$ steps! Conversely, if $C_{2k+1} \to C_{2m+1}$ existed with $k < m$, the image of the cycle would form a closed odd walk of length $2k+1$ inside $C_{2m+1}$—which must contain an odd cycle inside $C_{2m+1}$, impossible since the only cycle in $C_{2m+1}$ has length $2m+1 > 2k+1$!
6. **Cores (statement only)**: The smallest subgraph onto which $G$ maps homomorphically is unique up to isomorphism and called the **core** of $G$ (for instance, every bipartite graph with an edge has core $K_2$).

## 2. Counting Homomorphisms From Trees

Counting $\operatorname{hom}(G, H)$ for a general loopy graph $G$ is hard, yet when $G = T$ is a **tree** (no cycles!), each branch becomes independent once we fix its attachment vertex! Look how many famous counts drop right out:

- **Paths**: $\operatorname{hom}(P_{k+1}, H)$ chooses a starting vertex $v_0$ and steps along $k$ consecutive edges $v_0 - v_1 - \dots - v_k$—literally counting **all walks of length $k$** in $H$ (the sum of all entries of $A^k$!). For $k = 2$, the middle vertex $v$ can pick any neighbor left and any neighbor right, giving $\operatorname{hom}(P_3, H) = \sum_{v} \deg(v)^2$.
- **Stars**: In a star $K_{1,t}$, pick where the center goes ($v$), then choose images for the $t$ leaves independently among $N(v)$: $\operatorname{hom}(K_{1,t}, H) = \sum_{v \in V(H)} \deg(v)^t$.
- **Tree colorings**: Mapping a tree $T$ on $n$ vertices to $K_q$ picks the root's color in $q$ ways, and then each child's color simply avoids its parent in $q - 1$ ways, yielding $\operatorname{hom}(T, K_q) = q(q-1)^{n-1}$.
- **$d$-regular targets**: By the exact same root-down logic, if $H$ has $N$ vertices and every vertex has degree $d$, every $n$-vertex tree has $\operatorname{hom}(T, H) = N d^{n-1}$.
- **General Trees via Bottom-Up Dynamic Programming**: Pick any root $r$ of $T$. For each node $x \in V(T)$ and each potential target $v \in V(H)$, let $N_x(v)$ count homomorphisms from the *subtree rooted at $x$* into $H$ that force $f(x) = v$. At leaves, $N_{\text{leaf}}(v) = 1$. At any internal parent $x$, once $f(x) = v$ is fixed, every child subtree $c$ chooses an independent map sending $c \mapsto w$ for some neighbor $w \sim v$ in $H$:
  $$N_x(v) = \prod_{c \text{ child of } x} \left(\sum_{w \sim v} N_c(w)\right), \qquad \operatorname{hom}(T, H) = \sum_{v \in V(H)} N_r(v).$$

> [!NOTE]
> **Blakley–Roy Inequality (Lower Bound on Walks)**
> Does every graph with average degree $d$ have at least as many walks as a $d$-regular graph? Yes! The **Blakley–Roy inequality** (statement only) proves that any graph on $N$ vertices with average degree $d$ has $\operatorname{hom}(P_{k+1}, H) \ge N d^k$ walks of length $k$, with equality whenever $H$ is $d$-regular.

## 3. Counting Symmetries (Automorphisms) of a Tree

How many ways can you relabel a tree $T$ without changing which vertices are connected—i.e., what is $|\operatorname{Aut}(T)|$?

Recall **Jordan's Center** from Lesson 1.1: repeatedly peeling leaves leaves either one central vertex or one central edge, and **every** automorphism must preserve that center! Root $T$ at the center vertex (or if the center is an edge $u - v$, check whether exchanging $u \leftrightarrow v$ gives an isomorphism of the two sides, multiplying the rooted count by $2$ if symmetric!).

Working from the bottom up at each node, group its child subtrees into identical (isomorphic) types. If a parent has $m$ identical copies of child subtree $S$, any permutation of those $m$ identical branches ($m!$ ways) combined with internal symmetries inside each branch ($|\operatorname{Aut}(S)|^m$ ways) preserves the tree! Multiplying across all nodes gives $|\operatorname{Aut}(T)|$:

| Tree Example | Symmetry Structure | $|\operatorname{Aut}(T)|$ |
|---|---|---|
| Star $K_{1,n}$ ($n \ge 2$) | Root at center; $n$ identical leaf children | $n!$ |
| Path $P_n$ ($n \ge 2$) | Reverse ends only | $2$ |
| Spider with $3$ legs of length $2$ | Root at center; $3$ identical length-$2$ legs (each rigid internally) | $3! \cdot 1^3 = 6$ |
| Double star (center edge $u - v$, each with $2$ extra leaves) | Swapping $u \leftrightarrow v$ works ($2\times$); each center swaps its $2$ leaves ($2! \times 2!$) | $2 \cdot 2! \cdot 2! = 8$ |

## 4. Worked Examples & Python DP Solver

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Calculating $\operatorname{hom}(T, P_3)$ Step by Step**
> Let $T$ be the $5$-vertex tree with root $c$, two leaves attached directly to $c$, and one $2$-step leg $c - m - e$. Map $T$ into $H = P_3$ ($x - y - z$, degrees $1, 2, 1$).
> 1. **Leaves**: Every leaf starts with $N_{\text{leaf}}(x) = N_{\text{leaf}}(y) = N_{\text{leaf}}(z) = 1$.
> 2. **Node $m$**: Has one leaf child $e$, so $N_m(v) = \sum_{w \sim v} 1 = \deg_H(v)$. Thus $N_m(x) = 1, N_m(y) = 2, N_m(z) = 1$.
> 3. **Root $c$**: Has two leaf children (contributing $\deg_H(v)^2$) and child $m$ (contributing $\sum_{w \sim v} N_m(w)$).
>    At $v = x$: neighbor is $y$, so $N_c(x) = 1^2 \cdot N_m(y) = 1 \cdot 2 = 2$.
>    At $v = y$: neighbors are $x, z$, so $N_c(y) = 2^2 \cdot (N_m(x) + N_m(z)) = 4 \cdot (1 + 1) = 8$.
>    At $v = z$: neighbor is $y$, so $N_c(z) = 1^2 \cdot N_m(y) = 1 \cdot 2 = 2$.
> Adding across targets gives $\operatorname{hom}(T, P_3) = 2 + 8 + 2 = 12$ homomorphisms!

Let us check this exact DP calculation in Python:

```python
# Verify hom(T, P_3) = 12 via Tree DP for T = center c with two leaves and leg c-m-e
H_adj = {'x': ['y'], 'y': ['x', 'z'], 'z': ['y']}

# Leaf values N_leaf(v) = 1
N_leaf = {v: 1 for v in H_adj}
# Internal node m has 1 child e (leaf): N_m(v) = sum_{w ~ v} N_leaf(w) = deg(v)
N_m = {v: sum(N_leaf[w] for w in H_adj[v]) for v in H_adj}
# Root c has two leaves and child m
N_c = {
    v: (sum(N_leaf[w] for w in H_adj[v]) ** 2) * sum(N_m[w] for w in H_adj[v])
    for v in H_adj
}
print("N_c values:", N_c)
print("hom(T, P_3):", sum(N_c.values()))
```

```output
N_c values: {'x': 2, 'y': 8, 'z': 2}
hom(T, P_3): 12
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Homomorphisms Are Labeled Vertex Functions, Not Unlabeled Shapes**
> When $K_2$ maps onto $K_2$, swapping the two endpoints (`(u->1, v->2)` vs `(u->2, v->1)`) gives **two** distinct homomorphisms! Always count exact functions $V(G) \to V(H)$, not up to symmetry.

> [!TRAP]
> **Only Edges Must Map to Edges—Non-Edges Are Completely Free**
> Sending $P_3$ (`a - b - c`) onto a single edge $x - y$ via $a \mapsto x, b \mapsto y, c \mapsto x$ is a completely legal homomorphism even though $a$ and $c$ land on the same vertex! And for directed graphs, arrow directions $u \to v$ must land on $f(u) \to f(v)$.

## Exercises

2. How many walks of length $4$ exist in the $5$-cycle $C_5$? *(Hint: remember a walk of length $4$ is a homomorphism from $P_5$ into $C_5$, and $C_5$ is $2$-regular on $5$ vertices.)*

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. **No** from $C_5 \to C_7$ ($2 < 3$, shorter odd cycles cannot map onto longer odd cycles—the image would be a closed odd walk of length $5$ inside $C_7$, impossible!). **Yes** from $C_7 \to C_5$ ($3 \ge 2$): map vertices $0,1,2,3,4$ around $C_5$ once and bounce $4 \to 3 \to 4 \to 0$ back!
2. Using the regular-target tree formula on tree $T = P_5$ ($n = 5$ vertices) and $2$-regular target $H = C_5$ ($N = 5$ vertices, $d = 2$): $\operatorname{hom}(P_5, C_5) = N \cdot d^{n-1} = 5 \cdot 2^4 = 80$ walks!
