Imagine drawing dots on a page to represent cities, people, or game positions, and connecting pairs of dots with lines whenever there is a road, a handshake, or a legal move between them. That stripped-down picture—ignoring geometry, distances, and curved lines, keeping only *which dots are connected to which*—is a **graph**.

Almost every topic in Part I—finding a tour that visits every city, counting labelings, coloring maps, or proving Ramsey patterns—starts with the same core graph vocabulary and five fundamental structural theorems. Let us build them step by step, starting with precise words so nothing gets ambiguous later.

## 1. Everyday Vocabulary for Graphs

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Simple Graph, Degree & Neighborhood**
> A **simple graph** $G = (V, E)$ consists of a finite set of dots called **vertices** $V$ and a set of **edges** $E$, where each edge is an unordered pair $\{u, v\}$ of two *distinct* vertices (meaning no self-loops from a vertex to itself, and at most one edge between any pair).
> The **neighborhood** $N(v)$ is the set of vertices joined to $v$ by an edge, and the **degree** $\deg(v) = |N(v)|$ is simply how many edges touch $v$. If every vertex has the same degree $k$, we call $G$ **$k$-regular**.

Think carefully about the different ways to travel through a graph, because competitions love to test the subtle differences:

- A **walk** is any legal step-by-step trip from vertex to vertex along edges; you are free to revisit vertices and reuse edges as often as you like.
- A **trail** is a walk that **never repeats an edge** (though it may pass through the same busy intersection twice).
- A **path** is a trail that **never repeats a vertex** either.
- A **cycle** is a closed path of at least $3$ vertices returning to its start.
- $G$ is **connected** if some path joins every pair of vertices; the **distance** $d(u, v)$ is the length (number of edges) of the shortest path between $u$ and $v$.
- Selecting a subset $S \subseteq V$ together with all edges of $G$ whose both endpoints lie in $S$ gives the **induced subgraph** $G[S]$. Keeping the same vertices $V$ and swapping all edges with non-edges produces the **complement** $\overline{G}$.

Four named graph families show up in almost every problem set:

| Graph Family | Definition in Plain Words | Edge Count |
|---|---|---|
| Complete graph $K_n$ | Every pair among $n$ vertices is connected | $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ |
| Complete bipartite $K_{m,n}$ | Two groups of sizes $m$ and $n$; *every* cross-pair connects, no edge stays inside one group | $mn$ |
| Hypercube $Q_n$ | Vertices are all $2^n$ binary strings of length $n$; two connect iff they differ in $1$ bit | $n 2^{n-1}$ |
| Tournament | Start with $K_n$ and put an arrow (direction) on every edge | $\binom{n}{2}$ directed arcs |

## 2. Five Core Structural Facts

Now let us establish the five foundational theorems of Section 1. Notice how each one flows directly from a single simple counting idea.

### Fact 1: The Handshake Lemma

Suppose guests at a party shake hands, and afterwards you ask every single guest, "How many hands did you shake?" If you add up all their answers, each handshake gets counted **twice**—once by each person involved.

> [!THEOREM]
> **Handshake Lemma**
> In any graph $G = (V, E)$, the sum of all vertex degrees equals twice the number of edges:
> $$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|.$$
> Consequently, the number of vertices with **odd degree** must always be **even**.

Why the corollary about odd vertices? Because $2|E|$ is an even integer. When you add integers together and get an even total, you cannot have an odd number of odd addends!

### Fact 2: Bipartite Graphs and Kőnig's Odd-Cycle Theorem

A graph is **bipartite** if we can paint its vertices with two colors (say Red and Blue) so that every edge joins a Red vertex to a Blue vertex—no edge joins two vertices of the same color. Notice immediately that an odd cycle like a triangle $C_3$ or pentagon $C_5$ *cannot* be painted this way, because the colors must alternate around the cycle (`Red - Blue - Red - ...`) and clash where the last step meets the first. Remarkably, the reverse is true too: odd cycles are the *only* obstruction!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Kőnig's Bipartite Characterization**
> A graph is bipartite if and only if it contains **no odd cycle**.

> [!PROOF]
> If $G$ is bipartite, colors alternate along any cycle, so every cycle has even length.
>
> Conversely, suppose $G$ has no odd cycle. Color each connected component separately: pick any starting root vertex $r$, and paint each vertex $v$ according to whether its shortest distance $d(r, v)$ is even (**Red**) or odd (**Blue**). Could two vertices $u$ and $v$ with the same color share an edge? Suppose $uv \in E$ had $d(r, u)$ and $d(r, v)$ of the same parity. Follow a shortest path from $r$ to $u$, cross the edge $uv$, and take a shortest path backwards from $v$ to $r$. This creates a closed walk of length $d(r, u) + 1 + d(r, v)$, which is **odd**! And if an odd closed walk ever repeats a vertex, chopping out the loop between repetitions splits it into two smaller closed walks, at least one of which is still odd—continuing until no vertex repeats extracts an odd cycle, contradiction!

### Fact 3: Trees and Their Four Equivalent Faces

A **tree** is a connected graph with no cycles—think of a branching network with zero redundant loops. Because there are no loops, every single edge is vital, and you need exact edge economy:

> [!THEOREM]
> **Four Equivalent Characterizations of a Tree**
> For any graph $T$ with $n$ vertices, all four properties say the exact same thing:
> 1. $T$ is connected and has no cycles (acyclic).
> 2. $T$ is connected and has exactly $n - 1$ edges.
> 3. $T$ has no cycles and has exactly $n - 1$ edges.
> 4. Every pair of vertices in $T$ is joined by a **unique** path.

How do we prove things about trees without drowning in cases? Look at a longest simple path $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ in a tree with $n \ge 2$. Can $v_1$ have any neighbor outside the path? No—otherwise we could make the path longer! Can $v_1$ join another vertex $v_j$ ($j \ge 3$) inside the path? No—that would close a cycle! Therefore $v_1$ (and symmetrically $v_k$) has degree $1$: every tree on $n \ge 2$ vertices has at least two **leaves** (vertices of degree $1$). Deleting a leaf removes one vertex and one edge without disconnecting the rest or making cycles, which makes mathematical induction on $n$ effortless!

### Fact 4: Finding the Center of a Tree (Jordan's Theorem)

Imagine peeling the outer skin off an onion layer by layer. Take a tree and erase *all* its current leaves at once. The remaining graph is still a smaller tree! Repeat this layer-by-layer leaf peeling until you cannot peel any further. Jordan proved you always end with either a **single central vertex** or a **single central edge** (the **Jordan center**). Because every symmetry (automorphism) of a tree must map leaves to leaves, every symmetry is forced to map this center back onto itself—a trick we will use in Lesson 4.1 to count tree symmetries effortlessly.

### Fact 5: Tracing Every Edge Without Lifting Your Pen (Euler Trails)

Can you trace every edge of a drawing once without lifting your pen or retracing an edge? Every time your pen passes *through* an intermediate vertex, it enters along one edge and leaves along another, consuming $2$ edges at that vertex. Only where your pen **starts** and **ends** can an odd number of edges get used—unless the start and end meet!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Euler Trails and Circuits**
> Suppose $G$ is connected (ignoring isolated degree-$0$ vertices):
> - $G$ has a closed Euler trail (ending where it started) if and only if **every** vertex has even degree.
> - $G$ has an open Euler trail (distinct start and end) if and only if **exactly two** vertices have odd degree, and those two vertices must be the start and end.

## 3. Concrete Examples & Python Verification

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Testing Whether a Regular Graph Can Be Unbalanced Bipartite**
> Can a $3$-regular graph on $10$ vertices be bipartite with parts of sizes $4$ and $6$?
> By the Handshake Lemma, total edges equal $3 \cdot 10 / 2 = 15$. Suppose $V$ splits into part $A$ of size $4$ and part $B$ of size $6$, with every edge crossing between $A$ and $B$. Counting edges by looking at part $A$ gives $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ edges; looking at part $B$ gives $3 \cdot 6 = 18$ edges. Since $12 \neq 18$, no such bipartite split exists! In general, any $k$-regular bipartite graph with $k \ge 1$ must have equal part sizes $|A| = |B|$.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Counting Closed Walks in $K_4$**
> How many walks of length $4$ start at vertex $0$ of $K_4$ and end back at vertex $0$? Since the adjacency matrix $A$ of $K_4$ has eigenvalues $3, -1, -1, -1$, the total number of closed walks across all $4$ starting vertices is $\operatorname{trace}(A^4) = 3^4 + 3(-1)^4 = 84$. By symmetry among the $4$ vertices, exactly $84 / 4 = 21$ closed walks of length $4$ begin and end at vertex $0$.

Let us double-check that closed-walk count in Python by actually raising the adjacency matrix $A$ to the 4th power:

```python
import numpy as np

# Verify closed walks of length 4 in K4 via A^4
A = np.ones((4, 4), dtype=int) - np.eye(4, dtype=int)
A4 = np.linalg.matrix_power(A, 4)
print("Diagonal of A^4 (closed walks per vertex):", np.diag(A4).tolist())
print("Total closed walks trace(A^4):", int(np.trace(A4)))
print("Eigenvalue formula 3^4 + 3*(-1)^4:", 3**4 + 3 * (-1)**4)
```

```output
Diagonal of A^4 (closed walks per vertex): [21, 21, 21, 21]
Total closed walks trace(A^4): 84
Eigenvalue formula 3^4 + 3*(-1)^4: 84
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Connected Alone or Acyclic Alone Is Not a Tree**
> Being connected does not make a graph a tree (a triangle is connected!). Having no cycles does not make a graph a tree either (two separate edges form a disconnected *forest*). Always pair either connectedness or acyclicity with the exact edge count $|E| = n - 1$.

> [!TRAP]
> **Remember: Matrix Powers Count Walks, Never Simple Paths**
> The $21$ closed walks of length $4$ from vertex $0$ in $K_4$ include walks like $0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 2 \to 0$ that bounce back through $0$. Only closed simple cycles of length $3$ exclude repetitions automatically.

## Exercises

1. A simple graph on $7$ vertices has degrees $4, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1$. How many edges does it have?
2. At a party of $9$ people, can every person shake hands with *exactly* $3$ other people? Explain why or why not in one line.
3. Prove that in any tree with $n \ge 2$ vertices where the maximum degree is $\Delta$, there must be at least $\Delta$ leaves. *(Hint: use the Handshake Lemma!)*

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1, 2, and 3</summary>

1. Sum the degrees: $4 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 16$. By the Handshake Lemma ($2|E| = 16$), the graph has $|E| = 8$ edges.
2. Impossible! Every person having degree $3$ would give $9$ vertices of odd degree—whose degree sum $9 \times 3 = 27$ is odd, violating the Handshake Lemma ($2|E|$ must be even).
3. Suppose a tree has $k$ leaves and one vertex of degree $\Delta$. Every other vertex (besides leaves and the $\Delta$-vertex) is neither isolated nor a leaf, so it has degree at least $2$. Summing degrees over all $n$ vertices:
   $$2(n - 1) = \sum_{v} \deg(v) \ge k \cdot 1 + \Delta + (n - k - 1) \cdot 2 = 2n - 2 - k + \Delta.$$
   Subtracting $2n - 2$ from both sides gives $0 \ge \Delta - k$, so $k \ge \Delta$.

</details>
