In Lesson 1.1 we learned the basic anatomy of a graph. Now we ask three practical questions that turn up in network design, tournament scheduling, and chip layout:
1. Can every worker in group $X$ be paired with a distinct compatible job in group $Y$? (**Hall's Marriage Theorem**)
2. How many edges can we pack into a network before three vertices accidentally form a closed triangle? (**Mantel's and Turán's Theorems**)
3. When can we draw a network flat on a sheet of paper without any wires crossing? (**Euler's Planarity Formula**)

## 1. Pairing Every Vertex on One Side: Hall's Marriage Theorem (Fact 6)

Suppose $X$ is a group of applicants and $Y$ is a set of available jobs, with edges linking applicants to jobs they are qualified for. A **matching covering $X$** chooses one distinct job for every applicant.

Clearly, if any group $S$ of $3$ applicants collectively qualify for only $2$ total jobs between them, pairing them up is hopeless! Hall's theorem tells us that this obvious bottleneck is the *only* obstruction in the entire universe.

> [!THEOREM]
> **Hall's Matching Theorem**
> A bipartite graph with parts $X$ and $Y$ has a matching covering every vertex of $X$ if and only if for every subset $S \subseteq X$, its combined neighborhood in $Y$ is at least as large as $S$:
> $$|N(S)| \ge |S| \qquad \text{for all } S \subseteq X.$$

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Why Every Regular Bipartite Graph Splits Into Perfect Matchings**
> Suppose a bipartite graph $G$ between $X$ and $Y$ is $k$-regular ($k \ge 1$). Take any subset $S \subseteq X$. How many edges leave $S$? Exactly $k|S|$. Since every one of those edges must land inside $N(S)$, and each vertex in $N(S)$ can receive at most $k$ edges in total, we must have $k|S| \le k|N(S)|$. Dividing by $k \ge 1$ immediately yields $|N(S)| \ge |S|$!
> By Hall's theorem, $G$ contains a perfect matching $M_1$. Removing the edges of $M_1$ drops every vertex's degree by $1$, leaving a $(k-1)$-regular bipartite graph. Repeating this peel $k$ times decomposes every $k$-regular bipartite graph into $k$ edge-disjoint perfect matchings! *(This exact Hall-matching engine powers CMI Inter-IIT Problem 4 in Lesson 19.2.)*

## 2. How Dense Can a Triangle-Free Graph Be? Mantel & Turán (Fact 7)

Think about drawing $n$ dots and adding as many edges as possible without ever creating a triangle ($K_3$). One clever trick is right in front of us: split the $n$ dots as evenly as possible into two groups of sizes $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ and $\lceil n/2 \rceil$, and connect *every* dot in the left group to *every* dot in the right group (a bipartite graph!). Since bipartite graphs have no odd cycles at all (Lesson 1.1), this has zero triangles and achieves $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor \lceil n/2 \rceil = \lfloor n^2 / 4 \rfloor$ edges. Could a more twisted non-bipartite pattern beat that count? Mantel proved in 1907 that it never can.

> [!THEOREM]
> **Mantel's Theorem**
> Every triangle-free simple graph on $n$ vertices has at most $\lfloor n^2 / 4 \rfloor$ edges, with equality achieved by the balanced complete bipartite graph $K_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor, \lceil n/2 \rceil}$.

Let us watch how cleanly **Cauchy-Schwarz** (from Toolkit Lesson 0.2) proves Mantel's theorem in four lines:

> [!PROOF]
> Pick any edge $uv \in E$. Because there are no triangles in $G$, $u$ and $v$ cannot share *any* common neighbor! That means the neighbor sets $N(u)$ and $N(v)$ are disjoint subsets of the $n$ vertices, so:
> $$\deg(u) + \deg(v) \le n.$$
> Now add that inequality up over every single edge $uv \in E$! On the right side, the constant $n$ gets added once per edge, giving $n|E|$. On the left side, how many times does $\deg(v)$ appear in the sum? Once for each edge incident to $v$—that is, $\deg(v)$ times! Therefore:
> $$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)^2 = \sum_{uv \in E} \big(\deg(u) + \deg(v)\big) \le n|E|.$$
> On the other hand, applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the list of degrees and $(1, 1, \dots, 1)$ gives:
> $$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)^2 \ge \frac{\left(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)\right)^2}{n} = \frac{(2|E|)^2}{n} = \frac{4|E|^2}{n}.$$
> Comparing both ends gives $\frac{4|E|^2}{n} \le n|E|$. Dividing by $|E|$ (for $|E| > 0$) and multiplying by $n/4$ gives $|E| \le \frac{n^2}{4}$; since $|E|$ is an integer, $|E| \le \lfloor n^2 / 4 \rfloor$.

> [!NOTE]
> **Turán's Theorem (Generalizing Mantel to Larger Cliques)**
> Suppose instead of forbidding triangles ($K_3$), we forbid $r+1$ mutually connected vertices ($K_{r+1}$). Partitioning the $n$ vertices into $r$ nearly equal groups gives a graph with roughly $\left(1 - \frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{n^2}{2}$ edges and no $K_{r+1}$. **Turán's Theorem** states that this balanced complete $r$-partite graph is uniquely optimal. Notice plugging $r = 2$ gives $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n^2}{2} = \frac{n^2}{4}$, recovering Mantel!

## 3. Drawing Graphs Without Crossings: Planarity & Euler's Formula (Fact 8)

Can you lay out a printed circuit board on a flat table so none of the wires cross? A graph that can be drawn in the plane without crossing edges is called **planar**. Any such drawing chops the flat plane into connected regions called **faces**—including the vast unbounded region surrounding the whole drawing (the **outer face**).

Start with a tiny check: draw a single triangle ($V = 3, E = 3, F = 2$: inside and outside). Notice $V - E + F = 3 - 3 + 2 = 2$. Attach a fourth dot with one edge ($V=4, E=4, F=2 \implies 4 - 4 + 2 = 2$). Connect two existing dots to split a face ($E$ increases by $1$ and $F$ increases by $1 \implies V - E + F$ stays $2$!). Every way of growing a connected drawing preserves this invariant:

> [!THEOREM]
> **Euler's Formula & Planar Edge Bounds**
> Every connected planar drawing satisfies:
> $$V - E + F = 2.$$
> Furthermore, if $V \ge 3$:
> 1. Every planar graph satisfies $E \le 3V - 6$.
> 2. Every **triangle-free** planar graph (such as a bipartite graph) satisfies the tighter bound $E \le 2V - 4$.

Why do those two edge bounds follow so fast? In a simple graph ($V \ge 3$), the boundary of every face walks around at least $3$ edges, while each edge borders at most $2$ faces (one on each side). Adding up face boundary lengths counts every edge at most twice, so $3F \le 2E$, or $F \le \frac{2}{3}E$. Substituting into $V - E + F = 2$ gives $2 = V - E + F \le V - \frac{1}{3}E$, which rearranges immediately to $E \le 3V - 6$! When triangles are forbidden, every face needs at least $4$ edges ($4F \le 2E \implies F \le E/2$), which upgrades the bound to $E \le 2V - 4$.

![Planar Graph Obstructions K5 and K3,3](diagrams/1.2-planar-obstructions.svg "K5 violates E <= 3V - 6; triangle-free K3,3 violates E <= 2V - 4")

Look at our two classic troublemakers right away:
- **$K_5$** has $V = 5$ and $E = 10$. The planar limit is $3(5) - 6 = 9 < 10$, so $K_5$ **cannot** be drawn flat without crossings.
- **$K_{3,3}$** (three houses wired to water, gas, and electricity) is bipartite, so it has **no triangles**! Here $V = 6$ and $E = 9$, exceeding the triangle-free limit $2(6) - 4 = 8 < 9$. Hence $K_{3,3}$ is **non-planar** too.

## 4. Comparing the Bounds Across Small Graphs

Let us run a small Python check comparing Mantel's triangle-free limit against the two planar edge ceilings for $n = 3$ up to $10$. Notice an interesting transition around $n = 9$: for small graphs ($n \le 8$), planarity is *not* forced solely by being triangle-free, whereas by $n = 9$ a balanced bipartite graph has $20$ edges while a planar graph can fit $21$ edges—Wait: look at the triangle-free planar column! $K_{4,5}$ has $20$ edges, way above $2(9) - 4 = 14$.

```python
for n in range(3, 11):
    mantel = (n * n) // 4
    planar_max = 3 * n - 6
    bipartite_planar_max = 2 * n - 4
    print(f"n={n:2d}: Mantel(K3-free)={mantel:2d}, Planar={planar_max:2d}, Triangle-free planar={bipartite_planar_max:2d}")
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Euler's Formula Assumes the Graph Is Connected**
> If your plane drawing breaks into $c$ separate pieces (components), they all share the same outer infinite face! So the general formula becomes $V - E + F = 1 + c$. Checking connectedness first avoids off-by-$(c-1)$ errors.

> [!TRAP]
> **Always Count the Outer Infinite Region as One Face**
> When drawing a single cycle $C_4$ on a page, it divides the plane into $F = 2$ faces (inside the square and outside it), not $1$. Check: $4 - 4 + 2 = 2$.

## Exercises

1. A connected planar graph has $8$ vertices, all of degree $3$. How many edges and how many faces does it have?
2. Can a simple graph on $8$ vertices with $17$ edges avoid triangles completely?
3. Show that every connected simple planar graph has at least one vertex of degree $\le 5$. *(Hint: combine $E \le 3V - 6$ with the Handshake Lemma!)*

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1, 2, and 3</summary>

1. By the Handshake Lemma, $2E = 8 \times 3 = 24 \implies E = 12$. By Euler's formula ($V - E + F = 2$), $8 - 12 + F = 2 \implies F = 6$ faces (for example, a cube!).
2. No. By Mantel's Theorem, the maximum number of edges in a triangle-free graph on $8$ vertices is $\lfloor 8^2 / 4 \rfloor = 16$. With $17$ edges, at least one triangle is guaranteed.
3. For $V \le 2$ every vertex has degree $\le 1 \le 5$. For $V \ge 3$, $E \le 3V - 6$, so the Handshake Lemma gives $\sum_{v} \deg(v) = 2E \le 6V - 12$. Thus the *average* vertex degree is $\frac{2E}{V} \le 6 - \frac{12}{V} < 6$. Since the average is strictly less than $6$, at least one vertex must have degree strictly less than $6$, hence $\le 5$. (This is why every planar graph can be $6$-colored by simple induction!)

</details>
