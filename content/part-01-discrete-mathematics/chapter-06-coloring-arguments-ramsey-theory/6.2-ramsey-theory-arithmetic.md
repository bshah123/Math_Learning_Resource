Look up at a random star field or shuffle a deck of numbers: total chaos feels like the opposite of structure. Yet **Ramsey theory**—famous for Theodore Motzkin's motto *"complete disorder is impossible"*—proves that once a system gets big enough, you *cannot avoid* creating an orderly monochromatic pattern, no matter how cleverly you scramble the colors!

Every party puzzle ("Among $6$ strangers, $3$ already know each other or $3$ are mutual strangers") is the tiniest case of a general mathematical machine. In this lesson we derive the fundamental Ramsey bounds, watch Erdős's probabilistic coin-flip argument produce huge pattern-free colorings without ever building one by hand, and connect graph Ramsey to integers (**Schur**, **van der Waerden**, and **Erdős–Szekeres**).

## 1. Ramsey Numbers $R(s, t)$ and the Party Problem

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Two-Color Ramsey Number $R(s, t)$**
> The **Ramsey number** $R(s, t)$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that *no matter how* you color every edge of $K_n$ red or blue, there is guaranteed to be either a **red $K_s$** (all $\binom{s}{2}$ edges red) or a **blue $K_t$** (all $\binom{t}{2}$ edges blue).

By swapping the colors red and blue we always have $R(s, t) = R(t, s)$, and checking $s = 2$ gives $R(2, t) = t$ immediately (either some edge is red, giving a red $K_2$, or *no* edge is red, making the entire $K_t$ blue!). Now how can we prove $R(s, t)$ is finite for *all* $s, t$? By watching what happens from a single vertex's point of view!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Ramsey's Recursion & Binomial Upper Bound**
> For all $s, t \ge 2$:
> $$R(s, t) \le R(s - 1, t) + R(s, t - 1), \qquad R(s, t) \le \binom{s + t - 2}{s - 1}.$$
> Moreover, if **both** $R(s-1, t)$ and $R(s, t-1)$ are even integers, the upper bound sharpens by $1$:
> $$R(s, t) \le R(s - 1, t) + R(s, t - 1) - 1.$$

> [!PROOF]
> Let $n = R(s - 1, t) + R(s, t - 1)$, and take any red/blue coloring of $K_n$. Pick one vertex $v$. Its $n - 1$ edges split into red neighbors and blue neighbors. Because $(R(s-1,t) - 1) + (R(s,t-1) - 1) = n - 2 < n - 1$, by the Pigeonhole Principle either $v$ has at least $R(s - 1, t)$ **red** neighbors or at least $R(s, t - 1)$ **blue** neighbors.
> - If $v$ has $\ge R(s-1, t)$ red neighbors, that neighbor set already contains either a blue $K_t$ (we win!) or a red $K_{s-1}$—and joining $v$ along its red edges completes a **red $K_s$**!
> - Symmetrically, $\ge R(s, t - 1)$ blue neighbors force a red $K_s$ or complete a **blue $K_t$**.
> Now why do we save $1$ vertex when both $R(s-1, t)$ and $R(s, t-1)$ are even? Suppose $K_{n-1}$ avoided a red $K_s$ and a blue $K_t$. Then no vertex could have $\ge R(s-1,t)$ red edges or $\ge R(s, t-1)$ blue edges among its $n - 2$ neighbors, forcing **every** vertex to have red degree **exactly** $R(s-1, t) - 1$. Yet $R(s-1,t) - 1$ is **odd**, and $n - 1 = R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1) - 1$ is also **odd**—giving an odd number of odd-degree vertices in the red subgraph, impossible by the Handshake Lemma (Lesson 1.1)!
> The binomial bound $\binom{s+t-2}{s-1}$ follows at once by induction using Pascal's rule!

## 2. Erdős's Coin-Flip Bound & Goodman's Triangle Count

The upper bound $\binom{2k-2}{k-1} \approx 4^k$ grows exponentially. How big must $R(k, k)$ at least be? In 1947, Paul Erdős stunned combinatorics by showing $R(k, k)$ grows **at least exponentially** ($> 2^{k/2}$) without ever writing down a single explicit coloring!

> [!THEOREM]
> **Erdős's Probabilistic Lower Bound & Goodman's Bound**
> 1. **Erdős (1947)**: For all $k \ge 3$, $R(k, k) > 2^{k/2}$.
> 2. **Goodman's Formula (1959)**: Any red/blue coloring of $K_n$ contains at least:
> $$\binom{n}{3} - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \left\lfloor \left(\frac{n - 1}{2}\right)^2 \right\rfloor \right\rfloor$$
> monochromatic triangles. *(For $n = 6$: $20 - 18 = 2$, so every 6-person party actually has at least **two** monochromatic triangles!)*

Let us see the magic behind both proofs:
- **Erdős's Proof**: Color every edge of $K_n$ independently red or blue by flipping a fair coin. Any particular $k$-subset of vertices becomes a monochromatic $K_k$ if all $\binom{k}{2}$ edges pick red OR all pick blue—probability $2 \cdot 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} = 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}}$. Summing over all $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets by linearity of expectation (Toolkit Lesson 0.2), the expected number of monochromatic $K_k$'s is $\binom{n}{k} 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}}$. Plug in $n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor$:
  $$\binom{n}{k} 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}} < \frac{n^k}{k!} 2^{1 - \frac{k(k-1)}{2}} \le \frac{2^{k^2/2} \cdot 2^{1 - k^2/2 + k/2}}{k!} = \frac{2^{1 + k/2}}{k!} < 1 \quad (k \ge 3).$$
  Because the average count across all colorings is strictly less than $1$, at least one coloring has **zero** monochromatic $K_k$!
- **Goodman's Proof**: Call two edges of *different* colors meeting at the same vertex a **bichromatic V** (angle). Look at any non-monochromatic triangle (two red edges + one blue, or vice versa): how many bichromatic Vs sit inside it? **Exactly $2$!** At any vertex $v$ with $r$ red edges and $b = n - 1 - r$ blue edges, there are $rb \le \lfloor (n-1)/2 \rfloor^2$ bichromatic Vs—wait: sum $rb$ across all $n$ vertices (tightened by parity), divide by $2$, and subtract from $\binom{n}{3}$! *(Statement of infinite Ramsey: even $K_{\mathbb{N}}$ colored in finitely many colors contains an infinite monochromatic clique.)*

## 3. Turning Graphs Into Numbers: Schur, van der Waerden & Erdős–Szekeres

Ramsey's theorem is not just about graphs—by coloring edges according to **differences**, it forces patterns inside ordinary integers!

1. **Schur's Theorem ($x + y = z$)**: Paint the numbers $\{1, 2, \dots, N\}$ with $k$ colors. Once $N$ is large enough (take $K_{N+1}$ on $\{0, 1, \dots, N\}$ and paint each edge $\{i, j\}$ with the color of $|i - j|$!), Ramsey guarantees a monochromatic triangle $i < j < l$ inside $\{0, \dots, N\}$. Define $x = j - i$, $y = l - j$, and $z = l - i$: all three differences share the exact same color, and $x + y = z$! (For $x = y$ allowed, the largest $N$ avoiding monochromatic $x + y = z$ with $k = 1 \dots 5$ colors are $1, 4, 13, 44, 160$. Let us verify $k = 2$: paint `{1, 4}` Red and `{2, 3}` Blue. Why can't we extend to $5$? Without loss of generality $1$ is Red $\implies 1+1=2$ must be Blue $\implies 2+2=4$ must be Red $\implies 1+3=4$ forces $3$ Blue; now if $5$ is Red it clashes with $1 + 4 = 5$, and if $5$ is Blue it clashes with $2 + 3 = 5$!)
2. **Van der Waerden's Theorem (statement only)**: For any $r$ colors and target length $k$, sufficiently long intervals $\{1, \dots, W\}$ always hide a monochromatic $k$-term arithmetic progression ($W(2, 3) = 9, W(2, 4) = 35, W(3, 3) = 27$).
3. **Erdős–Szekeres Monotone Subsequence Theorem**: Write down *any* $mn + 1$ distinct real numbers in a row. Must there be either $m + 1$ rising numbers or $n + 1$ falling numbers? **Yes!** Label each number $a_k$ with a pair $(i_k, d_k)$: the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at $a_k$, and the longest decreasing subsequence ending at $a_k$. Look at any earlier number $a_j$ ($j < k$): if $a_j < a_k$, appending $a_k$ extends $a_j$'s increasing chain so $i_k \ge i_j + 1$; if $a_j > a_k$, appending $a_k$ extends $a_j$'s decreasing chain so $d_k \ge d_j + 1$! Either way, no two numbers can ever share the same label pair $(i_k, d_k)$! There are only $mn$ possible label pairs with $1 \le i \le m$ and $1 \le d \le n$—so by Pigeonhole, the $(mn+1)$-th term must exceed $m$ or $n$!

## 4. Worked Ramsey Proofs & Python Paley(17) Verification

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Proving $R(3, 3) = 6$, $R(3, 4) = 9$, and $R(4, 4) = 18$**
> - **$R(3, 3) = 6$**: In $K_6$, vertex $v$ has $5$ edges; at least $3$ share a color (say red to $a, b, c$). Any red edge among $a, b, c$ closes a red triangle with $v$; otherwise $a, b, c$ form a blue triangle! Lower bound $R(3, 3) > 5$: color the outer $5$-cycle red and the inner star blue.
> - **$R(3, 4) = 9$**: Both $R(2, 4) = 4$ and $R(3, 3) = 6$ are even, so our Handshake-adjusted bound immediately gives $R(3, 4) \le 4 + 6 - 1 = 9$! To show $8$ isn't enough ($R(3,4) > 8$), place $8$ vertices in $\mathbb{Z}_8$ and color edge $\{i, j\}$ red iff $(i - j) \bmod 8 \in \{1, 4, 7\}$: red edges form a $8$-cycle with opposite diameters (zero triangles!), and checking any $4$ vertices finds at least one red edge (no blue $K_4$!).
> - **$R(4, 4) \ge 18$ via the Paley Graph**: On the $17$ residues $\mathbb{Z}_{17}$, color $\{i, j\}$ red iff $i - j$ is a nonzero square mod $17$ ($\{\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm 8\}$), and blue otherwise. Since $17 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $-1$ is a square mod $17$, so coloring is well-defined and self-complementary—and contains zero monochromatic $K_4$! Combined with $R(4,4) \le 9 + 9 = 18$, this proves $R(4,4) = 18$!

Let us verify by exhaustive Python check across all $\binom{17}{4} = 2380$ quadruples that `Paley(17)` truly has $0$ red $K_4$s and $0$ blue $K_4$s:

```python
from itertools import combinations

# Verify the Paley(17) coloring has NO red K4 and NO blue K4 -> proves R(4,4) >= 18
qr = { (x * x) % 17 for x in range(1, 17) }
print("Nonzero squares mod 17:", sorted(qr))

red_k4 = 0
blue_k4 = 0
for quad in combinations(range(17), 4):
    colors = {((b - a) % 17) in qr for a, b in combinations(quad, 2)}
    if colors == {True}:
        red_k4 += 1
    elif colors == {False}:
        blue_k4 += 1

print(f"Paley(17): red K4 count = {red_k4}, blue K4 count = {blue_k4}")
```

```output
Nonzero squares mod 17: [1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16]
Paley(17): red K4 count = 0, blue K4 count = 0
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Ramsey Counts Vertices, Never Edges**
> A red $K_s$ is an $s$-**vertex** complete subgraph where *every* $\binom{s}{2}$ edge between those $s$ vertices is red. Saying "every vertex has $s$ red neighbors" does not give a clique!

## Exercises

1. Using the known values $R(3, 5) = 14$ and $R(4, 4) = 18$, what upper bound does Ramsey's inequality immediately give for $R(4, 5)$? Does the even-even bonus apply?
2. Find a sequence of $4$ distinct integers that has *no* increasing subsequence of length $3$ and *no* decreasing subsequence of length $3$—testing the sharpness of Erdős–Szekeres at $m = 2, n = 2$ (which guarantees a length-$3$ subsequence at $2\cdot 2 + 1 = 5$!).

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. $R(4, 5) \le R(3, 5) + R(4, 4) = 14 + 18 = 32$. Both $14$ and $18$ are **even**, so the Handshake Lemma bonus shaves $1$ off: $R(4, 5) \le 31$! *(In reality $R(4, 5) = 25$, showing recursive upper bounds get looser as parameters grow.)*
2. Take `2, 1, 4, 3`! Longest increasing subsequences have length $2$ (e.g., `2, 4`), and longest decreasing subsequences have length $2$ (e.g., `4, 3`). Adding *any* 5th number guarantees length $3$ by Erdős–Szekeres!
