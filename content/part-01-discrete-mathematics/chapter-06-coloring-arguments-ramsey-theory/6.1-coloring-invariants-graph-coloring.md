Take a regular $8 \times 8$ chessboard, chop off two **opposite** corners (leaving $62$ squares), and ask: can you cover the remaining board cleanly with $31$ standard $1 \times 2$ dominoes? You could test thousands of arrangements by trial and error—or notice in three seconds that opposite corners always share the same color, leaving $30$ light squares and $32$ dark squares, while every domino covers **one** light and **one** dark square. Proof complete!

That trick—assigning colors or algebraic weights to board squares so every legal tile consumes a fixed balance, then comparing the whole board's sum against the tiles—is a **coloring argument**. Next we push this idea from black-and-white checkerboards to complex **roots of unity** (for general $1 \times k$ tiles), and then review the core bounds for **proper graph coloring** culminating in the Moser spindle.

## 1. Board Tiling Invariants: From Parity to Roots of Unity

Every board-tiling impossibility proof follows a three-step template:
1. Paint or label every cell $(i, j)$ with a carefully picked color or number.
2. Prove that **every** legal tile placement covers a constant color pattern (or sums to $0$).
3. Show that the whole board fails to match the combined total of tiles.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Can a $10 \times 10$ Board Be Tiled by $1 \times 4$ Bars?**
> Using $2$ checkerboard colors won't stop a $1 \times 4$ bar because every bar covers $2$ light and $2$ dark cells ($50 = 50$ matches!). Instead, paint cell $(i, j)$ with **diagonal remainder** $(i + j) \bmod 4$ using colors `{0, 1, 2, 3}`. Every horizontal or vertical $1 \times 4$ bar covers $4$ consecutive cells whose $(i + j) \bmod 4$ residues hit all four colors `{0, 1, 2, 3}` once each! So if $25$ bars could tile the $10 \times 10$ board, each color would appear **exactly $25$ times**. Yet on a $10 \times 10$ square, counting the four diagonal stripes gives $25, 26, 25, 24$ cells of colors `0, 1, 2, 3`—impossible! *(Alternatively, notice a $2 \times 2$ checkerboard color split fails here too? Even simpler: any diagonal periodic coloring stops $1 \times 4$! Wait: actually $(i+j) \bmod 4$ immediately forces $25, 26, 25, 24$!)*

What happens for an arbitrary $m \times n$ rectangle tiled by $1 \times k$ bars? Clearly if $k$ divides $m$ or $k$ divides $n$, you just stack rows or columns of bars. Does any clever mix of horizontal and vertical bars ever tile an $m \times n$ board when $k \nmid m$ and $k \nmid n$? **Complex roots of unity** solve it cleanly in one blow:

> [!THEOREM]
> **de Bruijn–Klarner $1 \times k$ Rectangle Theorem**
> An $m \times n$ grid can be tiled by $1 \times k$ bars if and only if **$k \mid m$ or $k \mid n$**.

> [!PROOF]
> Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i / k}$ be a primitive $k$-th root of unity (satisfying $\omega^k = 1$ and $1 + \omega + \dots + \omega^{k-1} = 0$), and give cell $(i, j)$ (for $0 \le i < m$, $0 \le j < n$) the complex weight $\omega^{i + j}$.
> - Every $1 \times k$ bar starting at $(r, c)$ covers cells whose weight sum factors as $\omega^{r+c}(1 + \omega + \dots + \omega^{k-1}) = 0$! So *any* tiled board must have total weight $0$.
> - Yet the total weight of the entire $m \times n$ rectangle factors cleanly into two geometric sums:
> $$\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^{i+j} = \left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \omega^i\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^j\right).$$
> Because $\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\omega^i = \frac{\omega^m - 1}{\omega - 1} = 0$ if and only if $k \mid m$, the product can be $0$ **if and only if** $k \mid m$ or $k \mid n$!

## 2. Proper Graph Coloring & Chromatic Bounds

Now lift coloring from grid squares to arbitrary graphs:

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Proper Vertex Coloring & Chromatic Number**
> A **proper $k$-coloring** paints every vertex with one of $k$ colors so that no two adjacent vertices share the same color. The **chromatic number** $\chi(G)$ is the smallest $k$ for which a proper coloring exists.

Four classic tools bracket $\chi(G)$ from below and above:

1. **Two fundamental lower bounds**: Every vertex inside a clique $K_\omega$ needs a distinct color, giving $\chi(G) \ge \omega(G)$ (clique number). Similarly, each single color class can hold at most $\alpha(G)$ vertices (an independent set with no internal edges), forcing $\chi(G) \ge n / \alpha(G)$.
2. **Greedy upper bound & Brooks's Theorem**: Color vertices one by one in any order, always picking the lowest unused neighbor color. Since a vertex sees at most $\Delta(G)$ neighbors, you never need more than $\Delta(G) + 1$ colors: $\chi(G) \le \Delta(G) + 1$. *(Statement only — **Brooks's Theorem**: every connected graph except complete graphs $K_n$ and odd cycles actually satisfies $\chi(G) \le \Delta(G)$.)*
3. **Planar graph coloring**: In Lesson 1.2 (Exercise 3), Euler's formula proved every planar graph contains a vertex of degree $\le 5$. Delete that vertex, $6$-color the remaining planar graph by induction, and pick one of the $6$ colors missed by its $\le 5$ neighbors—so every planar graph is effortlessly **$6$-colorable**! *(With a short Kempe-chain swap, $5$-colorability holds easily too; the celebrated **Four Color Theorem** states $4$ colors always suffice.)*
4. **Edge coloring (statement only)**: Painting *edges* so shared-endpoint edges differ requires either $\Delta$ or $\Delta + 1$ colors for any simple graph (**Vizing's Theorem**), and **exactly $\Delta$** colors whenever $G$ is bipartite (**Kőnig's Line-Coloring Theorem**).

### How Many Colors Does the Entire Flat Plane Need? (Hadwiger–Nelson)

Imagine trying to color *every single point* in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ so that no two points at exact distance $1$ ever have the same color! How many colors do we need?
- An equilateral triangle of side $1$ uses $3$ points mutually at distance $1$, ruling out $2$ colors.
- What about $3$ colors? Look at the $7$-vertex **Moser spindle** below (two unit diamonds $ABCD$ and $AB'C'D'$ glued at tip $A$ and swung apart until $CC'$ has length $1$!). Try $3$-coloring $A$ as Red: then inside diamond $ABCD$, $B$ and $C$ take Blue/Green, forcing $D$ to match $A$ (**Red**)! By symmetry inside the second diamond, $D'$ must match $A$ (**Red**) too—giving the adjacent pair $DD'$ (wait: $CC'$ has length $1$ when rotating around $A$—see the labeled top/bottom tips connected by the unit edge!) the exact same color, contradiction! Thus the plane needs **at least $4$ colors**. *(Remarkably, in 2018 Aubrey de Grey raised the lower bound to $5$, so the exact chromatic number of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $5, 6,$ or $7$!)*

## 3. Python Tiling Weight Check

Let us verify the complex root-of-unity formula from the de Bruijn–Klarner theorem on four board sizes: notice `10 x 10` leaves nonzero weight $2.0$, immediately killing $1 \times 4$ tilings, whereas `10 x 12` hits exact $0$:

```python
import cmath

def board_weight(m, n, k):
    w = cmath.exp(2j * cmath.pi / k)
    total = sum(w ** (i + j) for i in range(m) for j in range(n))
    return abs(total)

for (m, n, k) in [(10, 10, 4), (10, 12, 4), (6, 9, 3), (7, 7, 3)]:
    print(f"{m}x{n} with 1x{k} bars -> |weight| = {board_weight(m, n, k):.6f}")
```

```output
10x10 with 1x4 bars -> |weight| = 2.000000
10x12 with 1x4 bars -> |weight| = 0.000000
6x9 with 1x3 bars -> |weight| = 0.000000
7x7 with 1x3 bars -> |weight| = 1.000000
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Check Necessary vs Sufficient on Non-Rectangular Shapes**
> Using $(i + j) \bmod k$ proves impossibility cleanly when the color totals don't match. However, matching color totals does *not* automatically guarantee a tiling exists on holes or notched boards—there you may need Hall's matching theorem (Lesson 1.2) or boundary-path arguments!
