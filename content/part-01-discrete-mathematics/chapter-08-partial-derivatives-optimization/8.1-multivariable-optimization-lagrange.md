Imagine standing on a foggy hillside trying to find the lowest valley—and sometimes constrained to walk only along a fenced mountain trail. When a function depends on several variables $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ at once, ordinary single-variable $f'(x) = 0$ is no longer enough: moving uphill East-West while sliding downhill North-South puts you on a **saddle point** rather than a true minimum!

In this lesson we organize multivariable optimization into four straightforward steps: finding the direction of steepest climb (**gradient**), checking bowl curvature (**Hessian** and **Sylvester's criterion**), handling fenced paths (**Lagrange multipliers**), and measuring how the optimal height shifts when a parameter moves (**Envelope theorem**, used later to solve sealed-bid auctions in one line!).

## 1. Gradients, Directional Slopes & Curvature Matrices

- **Partial derivatives**: For $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}$ differentiates $f$ along axis $x_i$ while pretending every other coordinate is a frozen constant.
- **Gradient vector**: Packaging all partial slopes into one vector $\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)$ points directly along the direction of **steepest climb**! Walking in any unit direction $u$ changes height at rate $\nabla f \cdot u = \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta$. Along any curve $x(t)$, the multivariable chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dt} f(x(t)) = \nabla f(x(t)) \cdot x'(t)$ (and composing vector functions multiplies their Jacobian matrices: $D(g \circ h)(x) = Dg(h(x))\,Dh(x)$).
- **Hessian matrix**: Just as $f''(x)$ measures curvature in one variable, collecting all second partial derivatives into an $n \times n$ matrix $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$ captures multivariable curvature. Whenever second partials are continuous, **Clairaut's theorem** makes $H$ symmetric ($f_{xy} = f_{yx}$).

## 2. Classifying Unconstrained Peaks, Valleys & Saddles

If you stand inside an open domain at a local peak or valley, the ground must be completely flat in every compass direction—forcing $\nabla f = 0$ (a **critical point**). How do you tell valleys, peaks, and saddles apart once $\nabla f = 0$?

1. **In 2 Variables (The Quick Determinant Test)**: Compute $D = f_{xx} f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2$ (literally $\det H$!) at the critical point. If $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0$, both directions curve upward like a soup bowl (**local minimum**); if $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0$, both curve downward like a dome (**local maximum**); if $D < 0$, one direction curves up and one curves down like a horse saddle (**saddle point**); if $D = 0$, the second derivative is inconclusive along a flat trough and you must inspect higher-order terms directly.
2. **In $n$ Variables & Sylvester's Criterion**: Positive definite $H$ (all eigenvalues $> 0$) gives a local minimum; negative definite $H$ gives a local maximum; mixed signs give a saddle. Instead of computing eigenvalues by hand, check **Sylvester's criterion**: a symmetric matrix $H$ is positive definite if and only if all of its top-left nested determinants (**leading principal minors** $\Delta_1 = H_{11}, \Delta_2 = \det H_{2\times 2}, \dots, \Delta_n = \det H$) are strictly positive!
3. **Convexity bonus**: If $H$ is positive semidefinite everywhere on a convex domain, the surface curves upward globally (**convex function**), guaranteeing that **every local minimum is automatically the global minimum**!

## 3. Walking Along a Fenced Trail: Lagrange Multipliers & Sensitivity

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Gradients Align on a Constraint Curve**
> Suppose you must stay on the contour trail $g(x, y) = c$ while trying to climb as high as possible on height map $f(x, y)$. As you walk along the trail, your elevation only stops increasing when the trail runs perfectly parallel to a level contour of $f$! Since the gradient $\nabla f$ is always perpendicular to contours of $f$, and $\nabla g$ is perpendicular to the trail $g = c$, parallel contours mean **the two perpendicular arrows $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$ must point along the exact same line**!

Look at what that geometry translates into algebra: wherever $\nabla g \neq 0$, constrained extrema must satisfy:
$$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g, \qquad g(x) = c.$$
With several constraints $g_1 = c_1, \dots, g_k = c_k$, simply write $\nabla f = \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j \nabla g_j$. Notice what the multiplier $\lambda$ means practically: if you loosen the budget $c$ by $1$ unit, the optimal value of $f$ shifts at exact rate $\lambda$!

> [!THEOREM]
> **The Envelope Theorem (Optimal Value Sensitivity)**
> Let $V(\theta) = \max_x f(x, \theta)$ denote the maximum value of $f$ as a function of an external parameter $\theta$, attained at a smoothly varying choice $x^*(\theta)$. Then:
> $$V'(\theta) = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}(x, \theta)\right|_{x = x^*(\theta)}.$$
> In plain English: to differentiate the optimal value $V(\theta)$, **hold your choice $x^*$ fixed and only differentiate the direct dependence on $\theta$**! Why does the indirect ripple $dx^*/d\theta$ vanish? By the chain rule, $V'(\theta) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx^*}{d\theta} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}$, and because $x^*(\theta)$ sits right at the optimum, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0$, killing the first term completely!

## 4. Worked Examples & Python Verification

> [!EXAMPLE]
> **Three Archetype Problems**
> 1. **Classifying critical points**: For $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$, set $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3, 2y) = (0, 0)$, giving critical points $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$. The second partials are $f_{xx} = 6x$, $f_{yy} = 2$, $f_{xy} = 0$, so $D = 12x$. At $(1, 0)$, $D = 12 > 0$ and $f_{xx} = 6 > 0 \implies$ **local minimum**; at $(-1, 0)$, $D = -12 < 0 \implies$ **saddle point**.
> 2. **Maximizing product for a fixed sum**: Maximize $xyz$ for $x, y, z > 0$ subject to $x + y + z = 3$. Setting $\nabla(xyz) = \lambda \nabla(x+y+z)$ gives $(yz, xz, xy) = (\lambda, \lambda, \lambda)$, forcing $x = y = z = 1$ and maximum $1$ (recovering AM-GM cleanly!).
> 3. **Closest point on a flat plane**: Minimize squared distance $f(x,y,z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ to the origin subject to $ax + by + cz = d$. Lagrange gives $(2x, 2y, 2z) = \lambda(a, b, c)$, so $(x, y, z) = t(a, b, c)$ for some scalar $t$. Plugging into the plane equation $t(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) = d$ gives $t = \frac{d}{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$, yielding shortest distance $\sqrt{t^2(a^2+b^2+c^2)} = \frac{|d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}$.

Let us test that closest-point distance formula on the plane $6x + 3y + 2z = 6$ in Python:

```python
import math

# Verify Lagrange distance from origin to plane 6x + 3y + 2z = 6
a, b, c, d = 6, 3, 2, 6
t = d / (a*a + b*b + c*c)
x, y, z = t*a, t*b, t*c
dist = math.sqrt(x*x + y*y + z*z)
print(f"Closest point: ({x:.4f}, {y:.4f}, {z:.4f}), distance = {dist:.6f} (6/7 = {6/7:.6f})")
```

```output
Closest point: (0.7347, 0.3673, 0.2449), distance = 0.857143 (6/7 = 0.857143)
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Watch Out for Singular Corners and Boundaries!**
> Lagrange's rule $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ silently assumes $\nabla g \neq 0$ and smooth interiors. On a closed region like $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$, check interior critical points ($\nabla f = 0$), boundary multipliers ($\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$), **and** any sharp corners or cusps where $\nabla g = 0$!
