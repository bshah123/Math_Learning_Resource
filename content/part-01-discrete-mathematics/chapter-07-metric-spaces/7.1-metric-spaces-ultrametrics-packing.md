When we say two things are "close"—whether two cities on a map, two genetic DNA strings differing by one mutation, or two numbers agreeing modulo $5^3$—we are secretly using the same abstract ruler. Instead of committing to Euclidean straight lines, mathematics strips distance down to **three common-sense rules** (**metric axioms**), creating a single language that covers city blocks, error-correcting codes, evolutionary trees, and $p$-adic numbers.

## 1. The Three Metric Axioms & Eight Everyday Examples

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Metric Space**
> A **metric** on a set $X$ is a distance function $d : X \times X \to [0, \infty)$ satisfying three rules for all points $x, y, z \in X$:
> 1. **Positive definiteness**: $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$. *(If two distinct points are allowed to sit at distance $0$, call $d$ a **pseudometric** instead.)*
> 2. **Symmetry**: $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$ (going there equals coming back).
> 3. **Triangle inequality**: $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ (detouring through $y$ never shortens the direct trip).

Look at eight completely different worlds ruled by these three rules:

| Space | Distance $d(x, y)$ | Intuitive Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{R}^n$, Euclidean ($\ell_2$) | $\sqrt{\sum (x_i - y_i)^2}$ | Straight-line crow-flies ruler (round ball) |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$, Manhattan ($\ell_1$) | $\sum \|x_i - y_i\|$ | Walking along city blocks (diamond in 2D, octahedron in 3D) |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$, Chebyshev ($\ell_\infty$) | $\max \|x_i - y_i\|$ | Chess king moves (square/cube ball) |
| Binary strings $\{0,1\}^n$ (Hamming) | Number of differing bit positions | Shortest path on the hypercube $Q_n$ |
| Connected graph | Shortest path edge-count | Number of hops on a network |
| Any set (Discrete metric) | $0$ if $x=y$, else $1$ | Everything else is uniformly "far" |
| Integers/Rationals ($p$-adic) | $p^{-v_p(x-y)}$ (and $d(x,x)=0$) | Closer when $x - y$ is divisible by high powers of prime $p$! |
| Text strings (Levenshtein edit) | Min single-letter inserts, deletes, swaps | Spellcheck & DNA edit distance |

On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the three $\ell_p$ grid norms trap each other within crisp constants:
$$\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1 \le \sqrt{n}\,\|x\|_2 \le n\,\|x\|_\infty.$$

## 2. Building New Metrics & Reverse Triangle Trick

Notice two handy facts once a metric is in hand:
- Subtracting $d(y, z)$ across $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ and swapping $x \leftrightarrow y$ gives the **reverse triangle inequality**: $|d(x, z) - d(y, z)| \le d(x, y)$.
- **Safe vs unsafe transformations**: Sums ($d_1 + d_2$) and maxima ($\max(d_1, d_2)$) of metrics are always valid metrics, as are capped distances $\min(d, 1)$, bounded ratios $\frac{d}{1+d}$, and concave roots $\sqrt{d}$. Watch out: taking the **minimum** of two metrics can break the triangle inequality, and **squaring** $d^2$ almost always breaks it (test points $0, 1, 2$ on the real line: $(2-0)^2 = 4 > 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$!).
- Distance-preserving maps are **isometries** (translations, rotations, reflections, and glide reflections in the plane; Mazur–Ulam says surjective isometries between real normed spaces are affine).

## 3. Strange Worlds: Ultrametrics, Trees & Ptolemy's Inequality

What happens if we tighten the triangle inequality from "at most the sum" to "at most the **larger** of the two"?

> [!DEFINITION]
> **Ultrametric (Strong Triangle Inequality)**
> A metric is an **ultrametric** if for all $x, y, z$:
> $$d(x, z) \le \max\big(d(x, y), d(y, z)\big).$$

That one change makes geometry feel delightfully upside down:
1. **All triangles are isosceles**: Suppose a triangle has side lengths $a \le b \le c$. The strong inequality forces $c \le \max(a, b) = b$, so $c = b$! In an ultrametric space, **there are no scalene triangles—the two longest sides are always equal!**
2. **Every point inside a ball is its center**, and any two balls either don't touch at all or one swallows the other completely!
3. **Why the $p$-adic metric is an ultrametric**: For prime $p = 5$, how close are $1, 6, 26$? Notice $6 - 1 = 5$ ($d_5 = 1/5$), $26 - 1 = 25$ ($d_5 = 1/25$), and $26 - 6 = 20 = 5 \cdot 4$ ($d_5 = 1/5$)—the two larger distances tie at $1/5$! Why? Because writing $x - z = (x - y) + (y - z)$ means any power of $p$ dividing *both* $(x - y)$ and $(y - z)$ automatically divides their sum, forcing $v_p(x - z) \ge \min(v_p(x-y), v_p(y-z))$, i.e., $d_p(x, z) \le \max(d_p(x, y), d_p(y, z))$!
4. **Rooted Tree Picture**: Every finite ultrametric sits cleanly on the leaves of a rooted hierarchy tree where node heights strictly drop toward the leaves and $d(x, y) = \text{height}(\operatorname{LCA}(x, y))$ (such as strings with $d(x, y) = 2^{-L}$ where $L$ is their longest common prefix length!).
5. **General Tree Metrics & Ptolemy's Inequality**: Putting points at leaves of an unrooted weighted tree satisfies **Buneman's four-point condition** (statement only: among $d(xy)+d(zw), d(xz)+d(yw), d(xw)+d(yz)$, the two largest always tie!). Meanwhile in any Euclidean/inner-product space, all quadruples obey **Ptolemy's inequality** $d(x,z)d(y,w) \le d(x,y)d(z,w) + d(x,w)d(y,z)$ (which Schoenberg proved characterizes inner-product norms!).

## 4. How Many Points Can Fit? Nets, Hamming Codes & Integer Balls

Suppose you want to transmit $n$-bit radio messages so cleanly that even if up to $t$ bits flip due to static, the receiver can still uniquely repair every message! Each codeword needs a protective bubble (**Hamming ball**) of radius $t$ around it so bubbles never overlap.

- An **$\varepsilon$-separated** set keeps every pair at distance $\ge \varepsilon$. Add points greedily until no room remains: that maximal separated set is automatically an **$\varepsilon$-net** (within $\varepsilon$ of everywhere!).
- **Euclidean Volume Bound**: Inside the $n$-dimensional unit ball of $\mathbb{R}^n$, place disjoint balls of radius $\varepsilon / 2$ around an $\varepsilon$-separated set. Every small ball fits inside the inflated ball of radius $1 + \varepsilon / 2$. Comparing $n$-dimensional volumes ($\propto r^n$) limits the set to at most $\left(\frac{1 + \varepsilon/2}{\varepsilon/2}\right)^n = (1 + 2/\varepsilon)^n$ points!
- **Hamming Sphere-Packing Bound**: Around one $n$-bit word, changing $0, 1, \dots, t$ bits produces $V(n, t) = \sum_{j=0}^t \binom{n}{j}$ words inside its radius-$t$ ball. To correct $t$ bit errors, codewords need minimum distance $2t + 1$ so their radius-$t$ balls stay disjoint—capping total codewords at $2^n / V(n, t)$!
  Remarkably, two famous **perfect codes** pack the entire $2^n$ space with zero wasted gaps: the **Hamming $(7, 4)$ code** ($t=1$, $16$ codewords $\times V(7,1)=8$ gives $128 = 2^7$) and the **binary Golay code** ($n=23, t=3$, $2^{12} \times V(23,3)=2^{12}\cdot 2048 = 2^{23}$)!
- **Integer Lattice $\ell_1$ Balls** (counting city-block grid steps): In 2D, $|x| + |y| \le r$ holds $2r^2 + 2r + 1$ lattice points; in 3D, $|x| + |y| + |z| \le r$ holds $\frac{(2r+1)(2r^2 + 2r + 3)}{3}$ points ($1, 7, 25, 63, 129$ for $r = 0 \dots 4$). These exact octahedral counts return in Lesson 16.1!

## 5. Python Metric & Coding Bounds Verifier

Let us check the $5$-adic isosceles triangle on `{1, 6, 26}`, verify the exact Hamming and Golay perfect packings, and print the 3D $\ell_1$ octahedral counts:

```python
import math

def vp(n, p):
    c = 0
    while n % p == 0:
        n //= p
        c += 1
    return c

# 1. Verify p-adic distances for 1, 6, 26 at p=5
pts = [1, 6, 26]
for i in range(len(pts)):
    for j in range(i + 1, len(pts)):
        d = 5 ** (-vp(abs(pts[i] - pts[j]), 5))
        print(f"d_5({pts[i]}, {pts[j]}) = {d}")

# 2. Verify Hamming & Golay perfect code volumes and 10-bit Hamming ball of radius 2
print("V(10, 2) =", sum(math.comb(10, j) for j in range(3)))
print("Hamming (7, t=1): 16 * V(7,1) =", 16 * sum(math.comb(7, j) for j in range(2)), "== 2^7 =", 2**7)
print("Golay (23, t=3): 2^12 * V(23,3) =", (2**12) * sum(math.comb(23, j) for j in range(4)), "== 2^23 =", 2**23)

# 3. Verify 3D l1 integer ball counts for r=0..4
l1_3d = [(2*r + 1)*(2*r*r + 2*r + 3)//3 for r in range(5)]
print("3D l1 integer balls r=0..4:", l1_3d)
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Always Check Positive Definiteness!**
> Define $d(f, g) = |f(0) - g(0)|$ on continuous functions. It is nonnegative, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality—yet it is **not** a metric, only a **pseudometric**, because $x$ and $\sin x$ are two completely different functions with $d(x, \sin x) = |0 - 0| = 0$!
