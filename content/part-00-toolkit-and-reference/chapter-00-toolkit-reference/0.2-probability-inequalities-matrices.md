When a competition problem looks completely impossible at first glance—whether it asks for the average number of hats returned to the wrong owner, the maximum value of a cyclic sum, or the long-run behavior of a rumor spreading on a network—it almost always yields to one of four workhorse ideas: breaking averages into simple pieces, bounding sums with classical inequalities, reading graphs through matrix eigenvalues, or estimating large factorials.

This lesson gathers those four remaining tools from the **Cross-Cutting Toolkit** in plain language, keeping the focus strictly on pure mathematics competitions like the **Inter-IIT Math Bowl**.

## 1. Probability Without Independence

Many students assume that to compute averages cleanly, random events have to be independent. Not true! Expected values add cleanly *even when events clash heavily with each other*.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why Averages Always Add**
> Suppose $100$ guests check $100$ hats at a door, and the attendant hands them back in a completely shuffled order. Whether guest #1 gets the right hat obviously affects whether guest #2 does—they are *dependent*. Yet to ask "how many hats land on the right heads on average?", we simply give guest $i$ a score $X_i = 1$ if they get their own hat and $0$ otherwise. Each individual guest has a $1/100$ chance of getting their own hat ($\mathbb{E}[X_i] = 1/100$), and the expected total is the sum of the $100$ pieces: $100 \times (1/100) = 1$.

Here are the five probability identities that appear repeatedly across Sections 6, 12, and 18:

1. **Linearity of expectation**: $\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]$ holds **always**, with zero independence assumption needed. Writing any count as a sum of $0/1$ **indicator variables** $S = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$ turns a messy global counting problem into individual one-item probabilities (used directly to count broken matchstick pieces in Screening Problem 15).
2. **Variance of indicator sums**: Expectations add blindly, *variances do not*! When variables interact, expand every pair carefully:
   $$\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^N \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{1 \le i < j \le N} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j).$$
   Using this expansion shows why the variance of matching hats in a random permutation is $\operatorname{Var}(S) = 1$ for every $N \ge 2$ (Screening Problem 17).
3. **The Probabilistic Method**: Imagine flipping fair coins to color a huge graph. If the *average* number of bad monochromatic patterns in a random coloring is strictly less than $1$ (say $0.4$), could every coloring have at least $1$ bad pattern? No! Otherwise the average would be at least $1$. So at least one coloring must have **zero** bad patterns. This idea proves Erdős's lower bound on Ramsey numbers (Lesson 6.2).
4. **Order statistics on $[0, 1]$**: Drop $n$ numbers independently at uniform random positions on the interval $[0, 1]$, splitting it into $n + 1$ gaps. By symmetry, all $n + 1$ gaps have the same expected length $\frac{1}{n + 1}$, so the $k$-th smallest number $X_{(k)}$ sits after $k$ gaps and satisfies:
   $$\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}] = \frac{k}{n + 1}.$$
   Every symmetric auction revenue formula in Lesson 12.2 rests on this one identity.
5. **Law of Total Expectation (Tower Rule)**: You can compute an overall average in two stages—first condition on a situation $Y$, then average over $Y$: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]]$.

## 2. Three Classical Inequalities

Whenever an Olympiad question asks to prove an expression is always $\ge c$ or $\le C$, try one of these three inequalities before heavy calculus.

- **AM-GM Inequality**: The arithmetic average of nonnegative numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n \ge 0$ is never smaller than their geometric average:
  $$\frac{a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n}{n} \ge (a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n)^{1/n},$$
  with equality if and only if all $a_i$ are equal. *Plain clue*: whenever you want to lower-bound $x + \frac{1}{x} \ge 2$ (whose product is $1$), AM-GM immediately proves $\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx \ge 1$ when $f(x)f(1-x) = 1$ (Screening Problem 1).
- **Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality**: Comparing squared dot products against products of squared lengths gives:
  $$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\right)^2 \le \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right),$$
  with equality iff the vectors $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $(b_1, \dots, b_n)$ are proportional. *Plain clue*: when you have a sum of squares or a sum of fractions $\sum \frac{u_i^2}{v_i}$ (Engel/Titu's form), Cauchy-Schwarz combines the denominators cleanly—powering Mantel's Theorem (Lesson 1.2), Screening Problem 4, and CMI Inter-IIT Problem 2.
- **Jensen's Inequality**: A function $f$ is **convex** (bowl-shaped, curving upward like $x^2$ or $e^x$) if every chord lies on or above the curve. Because averaging inputs sits below averaging heights on a bowl, $f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$; for strictly convex $f$, equality forces $X$ to be constant.

## 3. Matrices, Graph Walks & Rayleigh Quotients

Turning a network or quadratic expression into a matrix often converts a messy structural puzzle into clean algebra:

- **Adjacency powers count walks**: Write a $0/1$ adjacency matrix $A$ where $A_{u,v} = 1$ if vertices $u, v$ are connected. Then $(A^2)_{u,v} = \sum_w A_{u,w} A_{w,v}$ counts all 2-step walks $u \to w \to v$! In general, $(A^k)_{u,v}$ counts **walks** of length $k$ from $u$ to $v$. Summing the diagonal gives closed walks of length $k$, which equals $\operatorname{trace}(A^k) = \sum_i \lambda_i^k$ (yielding $\text{edges} = \operatorname{trace}(A^2)/2$ and $\text{triangles} = \operatorname{trace}(A^3)/6$).
- **Markov chains & martingales**: A Markov transition matrix $P$ puts probabilities in each row (entries $\ge 0$, rows sum to $1$); $P^k$ holds the $k$-step probabilities. Whenever a weighted sum stays constant in expectation at every step (a **martingale**), the expected outcome at the end equals the starting value (Screening Problem 18).
- **Symmetric matrices & Rayleigh quotients**: Every real symmetric matrix $M$ ($M^{\mathsf{T}} = M$) has all real eigenvalues and an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors. Therefore any unit vector $v$ ($\langle v, v \rangle = 1$) expands in that basis, forcing the quadratic expression $\langle Mv, v \rangle$ to stay trapped between the smallest and largest eigenvalues:
  $$\lambda_{\min}(M) \le \langle Mv, v \rangle \le \lambda_{\max}(M).$$
  This single sandwich solves CMI Inter-IIT Problem 1 immediately. Furthermore, $M$ is **positive semidefinite (PSD)** iff $x^{\mathsf{T}} M x \ge 0$ for all vectors $x$, equivalently iff every eigenvalue is $\ge 0$.

## 4. Comparing Growth Rates & Large Factorials

How fast does $n!$ grow compared to $4^n$? Which wins as $n$ becomes huge: $\log n$ or $n^{0.001}$? Asymptotic notation strips away small constant clutter so we only compare essential rates:

- **Big-$O$, $\Theta$, and $\sim$**: $f = O(g)$ means $|f(n)| \le c \cdot g(n)$ for large $n$; $f = \Theta(g)$ means $f$ grows at the same order as $g$ from both above and below; and $f \sim g$ means the ratio $f(n)/g(n) \to 1$.
- **Hierarchy**: Any power $n^\varepsilon$ (even $\varepsilon = 0.001$) eventually beats $\log n$, and any exponential $c^n$ ($c > 1$) eventually beats every polynomial $n^k$.
- **Harmonic numbers**: $H_n = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$ grows like $\log n + \gamma + o(1)$, where Euler's constant $\gamma \approx 0.5772$.
- **Stirling's formula**: Approximates huge factorials and middle binomial coefficients cleanly:
  $$n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n, \qquad \binom{2n}{n} \sim \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.$$
- **Infinite geometric series**: $1 + r + r^2 + \dots = \frac{1}{1-r}$ whenever $|r| < 1$.

## Verification Script

Let us test both the order-statistic expectation formula by Monte Carlo simulation and check how close Stirling's central binomial approximation gets for $m = 5, 10, 20$.

```python
import math
import random

# 1. Order statistics check: mean of k-th smallest of n Uniform[0,1] draws
random.seed(42)
n, k, trials = 5, 2, 20000
samples = [sorted(random.random() for _ in range(n))[k - 1] for _ in range(trials)]
print(f"Order stat (n={n}, k={k}): simulated={sum(samples)/trials:.4f}, exact={k/(n+1):.4f}")

# 2. Stirling approximation accuracy for central binomial C(2n, n)
for m in (5, 10, 20):
    exact = math.comb(2 * m, m)
    stirling = (4 ** m) / math.sqrt(math.pi * m)
    print(f"m={m:2d}: C(2m,m)={exact:>12d}, 4^m/sqrt(pi*m)={stirling:12.2f}, ratio={exact/stirling:.4f}")
```

## Common Traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Walks vs Simple Paths in Matrix Powers**
> Never confuse a walk with a simple path! The entry $(A^k)_{u,v}$ counts all *walks* of length $k$—which are allowed to bounce back and forth across the same edges and revisit vertices multiple times.

> [!TRAP]
> **Asymptotic Equivalence Is Not a Finite Inequality**
> Writing $f \sim g$ means only that $f(n)/g(n) \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$. Look at the table above: $\frac{4^m}{\sqrt{\pi m}}$ is actually slightly *larger* than $\binom{2m}{m}$ for finite $m$, so $f \sim g$ never lets you write $f(n) \le g(n)$ or $f(n) \ge g(n)$ without checking the error sign!

## Exercises

1. A coin with Heads probability $p$ is flipped $100$ times in a row (not necessarily independent if we don't care—wait: suppose each coin is independent with probability $1/2$). What is the expected number of pairs of consecutive tosses that both show Heads? *(Hint: define an indicator variable $X_i$ for positions $(i, i+1)$, $1 \le i \le 99$.)*
2. Use Cauchy-Schwarz on $(a, b, c)$ and $(1, 1, 1)$ to show that if $a + b + c = 1$, then $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \ge \frac{1}{3}$.

<details>
<summary>Show Solutions to Exercises 1 and 2</summary>

1. There are $99$ adjacent pairs $(1,2), (2,3), \dots, (99, 100)$. For each pair $i$, the indicator $X_i = 1$ iff both tosses are Heads, which happens with probability $(1/2)(1/2) = 1/4$. Notice adjacent indicators $X_1$ and $X_2$ overlap at toss $2$ and are **not independent**, yet by linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{99} X_i] = 99 \times \frac{1}{4} = 24.75$.
2. Apply Cauchy-Schwarz with $(a_1, a_2, a_3) = (a, b, c)$ and $(b_1, b_2, b_3) = (1, 1, 1)$:
   $$(a\cdot 1 + b\cdot 1 + c\cdot 1)^2 \le (a^2 + b^2 + c^2)(1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2) = 3(a^2 + b^2 + c^2).$$
   Since $a + b + c = 1$, we get $1 \le 3(a^2 + b^2 + c^2)$, dividing by $3$ gives $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \ge \frac{1}{3}$.
