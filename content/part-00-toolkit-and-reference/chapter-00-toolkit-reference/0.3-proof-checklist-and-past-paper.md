In this short orientation lesson, we establish a five-point proof-writing habit based on common grading deductions, map the $23$ real problems from the **Math Bowl 2024 Screening Test** (18 problems) and **CMI Inter-IIT Tech Fest Mathematical Competition 2023** (5 problems) directly onto our chapters, and briefly clear up the viral meme exam paper attached to our materials.

## 1. The Five-Point Proof-Writing Checklist

Before signing off on any competition solution, run through these five quick checks—nearly every avoidable lost mark comes from skipping one of them:

1. **Quantifiers first**: Spell out *for all*, *there exists*, *at least*, and *exactly*. Confusing "there exists one $n$ such that..." with "for every $n$..." completely changes a theorem.
2. **Existence means building and checking**: Whenever a problem asks you to prove something exists, give an explicit formula or construction and *actually verify* that it meets every single rule in the problem statement.
3. **Impossibility needs an invariant**: Do not just say "I tried small cases and it never works." Name the number, parity, remainder, or coloring that never changes when a move is made, show why it never changes, and point out why the start and target have different values.
4. **Extremal answers need two halves**: If you claim the minimum or maximum value is $M$, you must prove two separate facts—First, every legal object achieves at most (or at least) $M$; second, one explicit example actually achieves $M$!
5. **Case splits must leave nothing out**: State in one sentence why your cases cover every logical possibility (for example, "$n$ is either even or odd").

## 3. A Quick Note on the Viral 2024 Exam Meme Sheet

![Humorous meme exam attached earlier for reference](/assets/previous-year-math-bowl-paper.jpg "Satirical 2024 exam meme featuring Collatz, Riemann Hypothesis, and Fermat's Last Theorem")

Alongside the two real competition PDFs (solved in full in **Chapters 18 and 19**), you attached the humorous "impossible final exam" image above. If you have ever wondered why mathematicians laugh when they see it, here is the joke behind each of its three questions:

1. **Problem 1 (The Collatz Conjecture)**: Take any positive integer; if even divide by $2$, if odd multiply by $3$ and add $1$. Does every starting number eventually reach $1$? Nobody on Earth knows—it has been unsolved since 1937! (On top of that, the meme contains a typo writing $a_n = 1$ instead of $\exists i,\, a_i = 1$.)
2. **Problem 2 (The Riemann Hypothesis)**: Another \$1,000,000 unsolved Millennium Problem, disguised with two playful errors: it writes the domain as integers $\zeta : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ instead of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, and asks for an "if and only if" condition even though $\zeta(1/2) \approx -1.46 \neq 0$ (zeros occur at $1/2 + it$ only for specific heights $t$, never at $t = 0$!).
3. **Problem 3 (Fermat's Last Theorem)**: Asks you to prove $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no positive integer solutions for $n > 2$, while *explicitly banning* the Taniyama–Shimura modularity theorem—the central pillar of Andrew Wiles's 1995 proof—and poking fun at Fermat's famous narrow margin quote!

## Quick Python Exploration: Collatz & Zeta Check

Even though the meme problems are famous open conjectures (or famously hard!), Python lets us explore small starting numbers in seconds and verify that $\zeta(1/2) \neq 0$ directly.

```python
def collatz_steps(n):
    steps, peak = 0, n
    while n != 1:
        n = n // 2 if n % 2 == 0 else 3 * n + 1
        peak = max(peak, n)
        steps += 1
    return steps, peak

for start in [7, 27, 97]:
    st, pk = collatz_steps(start)
    print(f"Collatz n={start:3d} -> reaches 1 in {st:3d} steps (peak={pk})")

s = 0.5
eta = sum(((-1) ** (k - 1)) / (k ** s) for k in range(1, 50001))
print(f"Approx zeta(1/2) = {eta / (1 - 2 ** (1 - s)):.5f} != 0")
```

```output
Collatz n=  7 -> reaches 1 in  16 steps (peak=52)
Collatz n= 27 -> reaches 1 in 111 steps (peak=9232)
Collatz n= 97 -> reaches 1 in 118 steps (peak=9232)
Approx zeta(1/2) = -1.45496 != 0
```

## Quick Self-Check Exercise

1. Suppose a competition question asks: *"Find the maximum number of bishops you can place on an $8 \times 8$ chessboard so no two attack each other."* Why is proving "at most $14$ fit because there are $15$ diagonals of one orientation and the two corner $1$-cell diagonals conflict... wait—no, check both corner diagonals!" only **half** of a complete solution?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer to Self-Check Exercise</summary>

Proving an upper bound of $14$ only shows you cannot place $15$ or more. By Item 4 of the Proof Checklist, an extremal answer requires **both** a proof that $> 14$ is impossible **and** an explicit construction of $14$ non-attacking bishops (for instance, placing $8$ bishops along the top row and $6$ bishops in the inner columns of the bottom row). Without the example, $14$ isn't proven to be tight!

</details>
